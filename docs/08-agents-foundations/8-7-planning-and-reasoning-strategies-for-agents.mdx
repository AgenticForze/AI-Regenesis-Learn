## TL;DR / Intuition

By this point in the course, chain-of-thought, self-consistency, tree-of-thought, and
least-to-most prompting have all appeared in Phase 5, and ReAct, Reflexion, and
Plan-and-Solve have all appeared in Chapter 8.3. Each was introduced on its own, as a specific
mechanism. This chapter does not re-derive any of them; it places them on one shared map so
their real differences and real similarities become visible at once, and it introduces the
two names still missing from that map, plan-and-execute and graph-of-thought. Every
strategy this chapter covers differs along exactly two independent questions. First, how
far ahead does it commit before acting: does it decide one step at a time, reacting to
whatever the last step returned, or does it produce a full plan upfront and then carry it
out? Second, what shape does its reasoning take: a single chain of steps, a branching tree
of alternatives, or a graph where branches can merge back together? Once a strategy's
position on both axes is fixed, most of what matters about it, its cost, its ability to
recover from a bad early decision, whether it even needs a real environment to run at all,
follows from that position rather than needing to be learned separately for each name.

## High-Level Explanation

Treat the two questions above as two axes and every strategy this course has covered lands
somewhere on the resulting map. Chain-of-thought commits nothing at all to an environment,
it plans and reasons entirely inside one model response, no tool calls, no observations, so
it sits at the fully deliberative end and, being a single chain of reasoning steps, at the
linear end too. ReAct sits at the opposite end of the first axis, deciding one action at a
time and reading a real observation back before deciding the next, while remaining linear,
one sequence of steps, on the second. Reflexion occupies the same reactive, linear position
as ReAct but adds a loop back to the start when an attempt fails outright. Tree-of-thought
keeps chain-of-thought's fully deliberative, no-environment character but trades its linear
shape for a branching one, exploring several candidate continuations rather than committing
to one. Graph-of-thought, introduced in this chapter, keeps that same deliberative,
branching character but changes what a branch is allowed to do: two branches can be merged
back into one. Plan-and-execute, this chapter's other new name, is the one strategy that
does not sit still on the first axis: it produces a full plan upfront, deliberative, then
executes that plan step by step against a real environment, and re-plans reactively the
moment a step's result contradicts what the plan assumed.

## Deep-Dive Explanation

### Two Independent Axes: When to Commit, and What Shape Reasoning Takes

The commitment axis is really a question about what a strategy is willing to assume before
it has evidence. A fully reactive strategy, ReAct, assumes nothing about what a later step
will find, because it has not decided on a later step yet, it only ever commits to the
single next action and reads a real observation before committing to the one after that. A
fully deliberative strategy, plain chain-of-thought, assumes everything upfront in the
sense that it never gets to revise its reasoning against real-world feedback at all, it
produces an entire line of reasoning in one pass with no environment in the loop. Neither
extreme is a strictly better place to be; a fully reactive strategy pays an
environment-interaction cost on every single step, one real observation per action, while a fully
deliberative one pays nothing per step but has no way to notice, mid-plan, that an
assumption it made two steps ago has already turned out to be wrong. The shape axis is a
separate question about how many candidate lines of reasoning exist at once and how they
relate to each other. A linear strategy, whether reactive like ReAct or deliberative like
chain-of-thought, maintains exactly one line of reasoning throughout; there is no
alternative path being held in reserve. A branching strategy maintains several candidate
continuations simultaneously and can abandon the weaker ones, which costs more compute, one
model call per candidate rather than one per step, but buys the ability to not have
committed early to a path that turns out to be a dead end. Crucially, these two axes are
genuinely independent: knowing that a strategy is deliberative says nothing about whether it
is linear or branching, and knowing that a strategy is linear says nothing about whether it
is reactive or deliberative, which is exactly why four fundamentally different strategies,
ReAct, chain-of-thought, tree-of-thought, and plan-and-execute, can each occupy a distinct
position on the same two-dimensional map rather than forming a simple ranked list from
worst to best.

A map with two independent axes has four quadrants, and it is worth naming what each one
represents rather than treating the axes as pure abstraction. Reactive and linear is the
quadrant for a strategy that trusts the environment to keep it honest one step at a time,
never planning further ahead than it has to, exactly ReAct and Reflexion. Deliberative and
linear is the quadrant for a strategy that trusts its own single line of reasoning to reach
a correct answer without ever checking that reasoning against anything external,
chain-of-thought's quadrant. Deliberative and branching or graph-shaped is the quadrant for
a strategy that does not trust any single line of reasoning enough to commit to it without
comparing alternatives first, tree-of-thought and graph-of-thought. The fourth combination,
reactive and branching, describes a strategy that maintains several live candidate action
sequences at once and prunes them as real observations arrive, which none of this chapter's
named strategies fully occupies on its own, plan-and-execute comes closest by branching only
in the narrow sense that a re-plan discards the remainder of one candidate plan in favor of
another, rather than exploring several candidate plans in parallel the way tree-of-thought
explores several candidate reasoning paths. That gap is not an oversight in this chapter's
map; it reflects that reactive branching is expensive in a way deliberative branching is
not, every additional candidate branch in a reactive strategy needs its own real tool calls
and real observations, not just its own model call, which is why production agents rarely
maintain more than one live action sequence against a real environment at a time.

### Plan-and-Execute: Separating Planning From Execution

Plan-and-execute is easy to confuse with Chapter 8.3's Plan-and-Solve prompting because
both names describe producing a plan before acting, and the confusion is worth resolving
precisely because the two are not the same kind of thing at all. Plan-and-Solve is a single
prompting technique: one model call devises a plan and a second, often the same call,
carries it out in text, with no tool use and no opportunity to revise the plan once real
information arrives, because there is no real environment in the loop to arrive from.
Plan-and-execute, as described by LangChain's own architecture for it, is a multi-call agent
architecture built from two distinct components: "a planner, which prompts an LLM to
generate a multi-step plan to complete a large task," and "executor(s), which accept the
user query and a step in the plan and invoke 1 or more tools to complete that task." The
planner's output is a concrete, ordered list of steps, not prose describing an approach, and
each step in that list is handed to an executor that can actually call tools and observe
real results, exactly Chapter 8.4's tool-calling machinery, one step at a time. What makes
this a genuinely different architecture from either Plan-and-Solve or ReAct is what happens
after a step executes: "once execution is completed, the agent is called again with a
re-planning prompt, letting it decide whether to finish with a response or whether to
generate a follow-up plan." This re-planning step is where plan-and-execute picks up
ReAct's core advantage, the ability to notice that reality does not match what was assumed,
without giving up the advantage an upfront plan provides in the first place, that "the LLM only plans for 1 sub-problem at a time" in a pure ReAct loop, which "may lead to
sub-optimal trajectories, since it isn't forced to "reason" about the whole task" before
starting. The trade is not free: plan-and-execute's own stated limitation is that it "still
is restricted by serial tool calling and uses an LLM for each task since it doesn't support
variable assignment," meaning steps still generally run one after another rather than in
parallel, and each step still costs a full model call regardless of how simple that step
turns out to be. What plan-and-execute buys in exchange is stated just as directly: agents
built this way can "execute multi-step workflow faster," can "offer cost savings over
ReAct agents" on tasks with many steps, because the plan is produced once rather than
re-derived turn by turn, and can "perform better overall... by forcing the planner to
explicitly think through all the steps" before any of them run, catching a poorly ordered
or logically inconsistent plan before it costs a single tool call to discover.

The planner and executor's separation also changes where a description problem, Chapter
8.4's and Chapter 8.6's shared concern about tool sets, actually shows up. A pure ReAct loop
has one component deciding both what the overall task needs and which specific tool serves
the very next action, so an ambiguous tool description degrades every single decision that
component makes. Splitting planning from execution narrows that exposure: the planner only
ever needs to know a task decomposes into steps like "find a flight" or "check the total
cost," not which specific tool implements either one, while only the executor, working one
step at a time, needs the finer-grained tool knowledge Chapter 8.4 covered. This means a
planner's plan can, in principle, remain valid even after the underlying tools change,
provided the new tools still cover the same step descriptions, whereas a ReAct loop's
single reasoning stream has no equivalent layer of indirection between deciding on an
approach and deciding on a specific call. The separation is not without its own failure
mode, though: a planner that names a step in terms the available executor cannot actually
fulfill, "negotiate a lower rate" when no tool supports negotiation, produces a plan that
looks coherent on paper and only reveals the mismatch once an executor tries and fails to
carry that specific step out, at which point re-planning has to correct a problem the
planner itself introduced rather than one the environment introduced.

### Graph-of-Thought: When Branches Need to Merge

Tree-of-thought, covered in Phase 5, lets reasoning branch: at each step, several candidate
continuations are generated, evaluated, and the weaker ones are pruned, keeping the
strongest paths alive for the next round. What a tree structure cannot do, by definition, is
let two branches that split apart earlier come back together; once a tree forks, each
resulting path is on its own for the rest of the search. Graph of Thoughts (Besta et al.,
2023) removes exactly that restriction by changing the underlying data structure: "the key
idea and primary advantage of GoT is the ability to model the information generated by an
LLM as an arbitrary graph, where units of information, LLM thoughts, are vertices, and edges
correspond to dependencies between these vertices." A graph, unlike a tree, permits a vertex
with more than one incoming edge, which is precisely what lets two previously independent
branches feed into a single new thought that combines them, an operation the paper describes
as "combining arbitrary LLM thoughts into synergistic outcomes," alongside two other
named capabilities a tree structure does not support, "distilling the essence of whole
networks of thoughts," compressing many prior thoughts into a smaller number without
discarding them the way pruning does, and "enhancing thoughts using feedback loops," revising
a thought using a later thought's evaluation of it, which requires an edge pointing backward,
something a tree's strictly branching structure has no way to represent at all. The paper's
own headline result on a sorting task makes concrete what this structural freedom is worth
in practice, not just in principle: GoT achieved "increasing the quality of sorting by 62%
over ToT, while simultaneously reducing costs by >31%," improving on both the
answer's quality and the compute spent reaching it in the same comparison, rather than
trading one for the other the way many of this chapter's other comparisons do. The intuition
behind why merging helps on a task like sorting is straightforward once stated: sorting a
long list decomposes naturally into sorting several shorter sublists and then merging the
sorted results, and a tree structure has no native way to represent that final merge step as
part of the same search it used to produce the sublists, forcing a tree-based approach to
handle merging as a separate, unscored step outside the reasoning process the tree itself
was tracking.

Whether merging is worth its added complexity depends heavily on what kind of problem is
being solved, which is why graph-of-thought is best understood as extending tree-of-thought
for a specific class of tasks rather than replacing it outright. A problem that genuinely
decomposes into independent sub-results needing later combination, sorting a list by
sorting and merging sublists, synthesizing several partial drafts of a document into one,
aggregating several independent estimates into a single figure, plays directly to a graph
structure's strength, because the combination step is exactly the kind of operation a tree
cannot natively score as part of the same search. A problem that is genuinely a single
branching decision tree, where each path really does represent a mutually exclusive choice
rather than a partial result meant to be recombined, gets no benefit from the extra
structure graph-of-thought provides, and pays the cost of a more complex reasoning
framework for a merge operation it never actually needs. The practical reason
graph-of-thought is introduced here rather than back in Phase 5 alongside tree-of-thought is that the
decision of whether a task decomposes this way is often only visible once an agent is
already working the task, which is squarely this course's territory rather than a
pure-prompting one: an agent midway through a multi-step task is frequently the one
best positioned to recognize that two of its own partial results, not two hypothetical
reasoning branches planned in advance, are what actually need merging.

### Reactive vs. Deliberative: What Each Strategy Assumes About the Environment

A distinction this chapter's map makes visible, but that is easy to miss when each strategy
is studied in isolation, is that not every strategy on it is actually a candidate for
Chapter 8.3's agent loop in the first place. ReAct, Reflexion, and plan-and-execute all
assume a real environment exists to act on and observe, tools that return real results,
because their reactive component is specifically a response to those real results; take the
environment away and there is nothing left for the reactive half of either strategy to react
to. Chain-of-thought, tree-of-thought, and graph-of-thought assume no such thing: every one
of them can run to completion with zero tool calls, zero observations, and zero interaction
with anything outside the model's own generated text, because they were designed to improve
how a model reasons about a self-contained problem, not to interleave reasoning with acting
in a world that talks back. This is not a hierarchy where the reactive strategies are
strictly more capable; it means the two groups are typically not competing for the same job
inside an agent's design. A concrete way to see this is to notice that the deliberative,
no-environment strategies are eligible to run inside a single Plan step of Chapter 8.3's
loop, an agent could use tree-of-thought or graph-of-thought purely to decide what its next
action should be, still handing that decision off to ReAct-style Act and Observe steps
afterward, whereas it makes no sense to describe running ReAct "inside" a chain-of-thought
step, because ReAct's very definition requires the real Act and Observe steps that a single
reasoning step does not have access to. Plan-and-execute complicates this picture in a
useful way rather than breaking it: its planner half behaves like a deliberative,
no-environment strategy, producing a plan without touching any tool, while its executor and
re-planner halves behave exactly like ReAct, one real action and one real observation at a
time, which is exactly why plan-and-execute was the strategy on this chapter's map that
would not sit still on a single point.

This split also determines which of Chapter 8.6's context-engineering concerns actually
apply to a given strategy. A deliberative, no-environment strategy's entire working set,
every candidate thought a tree-of-thought or graph-of-thought search maintains, lives inside
context for the duration of one call and is discarded once that call returns an answer;
none of Chapter 8.6's long-horizon techniques, compaction, structured notes, sub-agent
isolation, are relevant to a strategy that never runs long enough to need them. A reactive
strategy's context, by contrast, accumulates exactly the way Chapter 8.6 described, one
observation at a time across a run that can extend indefinitely, which is why ReAct,
Reflexion, and plan-and-execute's executor loop are the strategies actually exposed to
context rot and actually candidates for compaction or note-taking, while chain-of-thought,
tree-of-thought, and graph-of-thought simply are not, by virtue of not running long enough
in the first place for accumulated history to become a problem.

### Choosing a Strategy: Cost, Latency, and Failure Recovery

Every strategy on this chapter's map trades off the same three practical quantities
differently, and naming the trade explicitly is more useful than treating any one strategy
as a universal default. Cost, measured in model calls for an n-step task, is lowest for
plain chain-of-thought, one call regardless of n, because there is no environment loop to
pay for at all; ReAct and Reflexion cost roughly one call per step, since each action needs
its own reasoning call informed by the previous observation; branching strategies,
tree-of-thought and graph-of-thought, cost a multiple of that per step, one call per
candidate continuation being explored, before any pruning or merging reduces the surviving
set; and plan-and-execute costs one upfront planning call plus roughly one call per step,
occasionally more when a step triggers re-planning, landing between ReAct's per-step cost
and a branching strategy's per-candidate cost. Latency tracks cost closely but not
identically, because branching strategies can often evaluate their candidates in parallel,
several model calls issued at once rather than in sequence, while ReAct, Reflexion, and
plan-and-execute's executor are inherently sequential, each step's call needing the
previous step's real-world result before it can even be issued. Failure recovery is where
the strategies diverge most sharply from each other, and this is arguably the most
practically important axis of the three: plain chain-of-thought has no recovery mechanism
of any kind, an error introduced early in the chain propagates to every step built on top of
it with nothing to catch it; Reflexion adds recovery specifically for outright failures,
detected and corrected only at the level of an entire attempt via Chapter 8.3's reflect
step, not mid-attempt; branching strategies recover by never fully committing to a single
path until pruning or merging happens, at the direct cost of exploring multiple candidates
whether or not the eventual winner needed the alternatives at all; and plan-and-execute
recovers at the level of an individual step, re-planning precisely when that step's result
contradicts an assumption, which is finer-grained than Reflexion's whole-attempt recovery
and cheaper than a branching strategy's always-on redundancy, at the cost of only catching
problems a step's own observation actually surfaces.

None of these three quantities should be optimized in isolation, because the right balance
depends on what a wrong answer actually costs in the specific task at hand. A task where a
wrong final answer is cheap to detect and cheap to redo, drafting a short summary, favors
minimizing cost and latency over failure recovery, since plain chain-of-thought's total
inability to self-correct mid-reasoning barely matters when checking and regenerating the
whole output costs almost nothing. A task where a wrong intermediate step is expensive to
discover late, a multi-step booking process where an early bad assumption is only caught
after several more steps have already run and possibly committed real side effects, favors
exactly the opposite trade, paying plan-and-execute's or a branching strategy's higher
per-task cost specifically to catch the bad assumption closer to where it was made rather
than several irreversible steps later. This is also why no single strategy from this
chapter's map should be treated as a default: the right choice is a property of the task's
own cost of being wrong, not a property of which strategy happens to be newest or most
sophisticated on paper.

The loop only ever revises the plan's remaining steps, never the steps already executed;
re-planning is forward-looking by construction, because a tool call already made cannot be
unmade the way a purely deliberative strategy's untried candidate can simply be discarded.

## Hands-On: A Plan That Gets Revised, Not Just Followed

The clearest way to see what re-planning actually buys over a plan that can only ever be
followed is to give a fixed three-step plan a result partway through that violates one of
its assumptions, and watch the difference between a system that notices and one that does
not.

**Step 1: A plan, and the state it operates on.** The plan books a flight, books a hotel,
and checks the combined cost against a budget; nothing about the plan itself anticipates
that the hotel choice might need to change:

```python
FLIGHTS = {"flight": 450}
HOTELS = {"standard": 900, "budget": 500}
BUDGET_LIMIT = 1200

def make_plan():
    return ["find_flight", "find_hotel", "check_budget"]

def execute_step(step, state):
    if step == "find_flight":
        state["flight_cost"] = FLIGHTS["flight"]
        return f"Booked flight for ${state['flight_cost']}."
    if step == "find_hotel":
        state["hotel_cost"] = HOTELS[state["hotel_choice"]]
        return f"Booked {state['hotel_choice']} hotel for ${state['hotel_cost']}."
    if step == "check_budget":
        total = state["flight_cost"] + state["hotel_cost"]
        state["total"] = total
        if total > BUDGET_LIMIT:
            return f"Over budget: ${total} exceeds limit ${BUDGET_LIMIT}."
        return f"Within budget: ${total} is under limit ${BUDGET_LIMIT}."
    raise ValueError(f"unknown step: {step}")
```

**Step 2: A re-planner that only fires when the plan's assumption is actually violated.**
It checks the observation, not a fixed schedule, and returns corrective steps only when
needed:

```python
def replan(observation, state):
    """Returns a list of corrective steps to append, or [] if none needed."""
    if "Over budget" in observation and state["hotel_choice"] != "budget":
        state["hotel_choice"] = "budget"
        return ["find_hotel", "check_budget"]
    return []

def run_plan_and_execute():
    state = {"flight_cost": 0, "hotel_cost": 0, "hotel_choice": "standard"}
    plan = make_plan()
    history = []
    replans = 0
    i = 0
    while i < len(plan):
        step = plan[i]
        obs = execute_step(step, state)
        history.append((step, obs))
        if step == "check_budget":
            extra = replan(obs, state)
            if extra:
                replans += 1
                plan = plan[: i + 1] + extra
        i += 1
    return history, replans, state
```

**Step 3: Run it.** The initial plan assumes a standard hotel, which turns out to exceed
budget once combined with the flight:

```python
history, replans, state = run_plan_and_execute()
```

Running this executes five steps in total, not the original plan's three:
`find_flight`, `find_hotel`, `check_budget`, then, because the first `check_budget` result
was `"Over budget: $1350 exceeds limit $1200."`, a re-planned `find_hotel` and a second
`check_budget`, this time reporting `"Within budget: $950 is under limit $1200."`. Exactly
one re-plan fires, the hotel choice switches from standard to budget, and the final state's
total is 950. All of this was confirmed by actually running the code, including the exact
sequence of five steps and the exact dollar figures at each one, not predicted by hand.

The detail worth sitting with is what would have happened under Plan-and-Solve instead of
plan-and-execute, given the identical starting plan. Plan-and-Solve produces its plan and
carries it out within a single pass of reasoning, with no real tool call and no real
observation to notice that the standard hotel plus the flight actually exceeds budget until
the whole thing is already reasoned through; there is no step in that architecture
positioned to catch the problem and revise just the hotel choice, only to report a finished
answer that is wrong, or to restart the entire plan from scratch, which is the least
efficient way this chapter's map describes to recover from one bad assumption two steps in.
Re-planning is not "using tools" that Plan-and-Solve lacks; ReAct alone could catch the same
overage, one action at a time, but only after re-deciding everything about the flight step
too, since it never had a distinct kept plan for re-planning to leave untouched in the first
place. Plan-and-execute's specific advantage is narrower and more precise than either
alternative: it revises exactly the part of an existing plan that a real result invalidated,
and nothing else.
