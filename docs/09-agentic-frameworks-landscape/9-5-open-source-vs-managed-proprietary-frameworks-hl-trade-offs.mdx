## TL;DR

Every framework and platform named in 9.2 and 9.3 sits somewhere on two separate axes that
get conflated far too often: whether its code is open source, and whether someone else
operates it for you. This chapter names those two axes and the concrete trade-offs that follow
from where a given choice lands on each: cost model, customization ceiling, and switching cost.
Neither axis has a universal right answer; 9.6 turns these same dimensions into an actual
decision framework for picking a starting point.

## High-Level Explanation

"Open source" and "managed" are frequently treated as two ends of one spectrum, but they are
actually independent questions. LangGraph and CrewAI's core frameworks are open source and
self-hosted by default: the code is MIT-licensed, and you run it on your own infrastructure.
LangChain also sells LangSmith Deployment, renamed from LangGraph Platform in October 2025,
the same open orchestration logic wrapped in a managed deployment service, open source and
managed at once. Amazon Bedrock AgentCore and Google's Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform sit at
a third position, proprietary and managed: their internals are not published, and the vendor
operates the runtime. This phase's examples do not include the fourth combination, proprietary
code you host yourself, which is rarer in this ecosystem and closer to traditional on-premises
enterprise software licensing than to anything covered so far. A fifth pattern cuts across the
open-source-versus-proprietary axis rather than filling in a missing cell: CrewAI AMP is a
proprietary, managed control plane sold around CrewAI's own open-source core, the
textbook shape of what the software industry calls an open-core business model, "a business
model for the monetization of open-source software to which a for-profit company provides
significant development support." Knowing whether a paid layer sits beside an open core, rather
than assuming a product is simply "open source" or "proprietary," matters more than the label
alone.

The cost model follows directly from where a product sits on these two axes. Self-hosted open
source carries no license fee, but its real cost is the infrastructure and the engineering time
needed to run, upgrade, and secure it yourself, a cost that is easy to underestimate because it
never appears on an invoice. A managed or proprietary platform instead bills by usage, API
calls, or agent-runtime hours, trading a predictable line item for a cost that scales with how
much the system actually gets used. An open-core commercial layer, LangSmith and LangSmith
Deployment, or CrewAI AMP, charges specifically for observability, governance, and support
built around the free core, not for the orchestration logic itself, which stays open and free
either way.

Control and lock-in cut the other direction. Running an open-source framework yourself gives
full control over upgrade timing, data residency, and customization, at the cost of owning the
maintenance burden that a managed platform would otherwise absorb. A managed platform trades
that control for reduced operational burden, but it can raise the cost of leaving, the dynamic
generically called vendor lock-in, "when someone is essentially forced to continue using a
product or service regardless of quality, because switching away from that product or service
is not practical." A milder version of the same switching-cost dynamic shows up even within
one vendor's own product line: AWS's own guidance on moving off Amazon Bedrock Agents Classic
onto Bedrock AgentCore describes two distinct migration paths rather than a drop-in swap, work
a team only takes on because the alternative, staying on a maintenance-mode product
indefinitely, is its own cost. Lock-in is not exclusive to proprietary platforms; a team that
has written extensive code against one open-source framework's specific abstractions faces its
own switching cost. The difference is that the underlying code and data usually remain the
team's own to move, which a fully managed proprietary service does not always guarantee in the
same way.

None of this adds up to a universal winner, because the deciding factors are specific to a
team rather than to a product. A small team with limited operations capacity may prefer a
managed platform's higher recurring cost over the burden of running infrastructure itself.
Compliance or data-residency requirements can force self-hosting regardless of cost. And how
fast a team needs a working agent matters on its own terms: AWS frames its AgentCore harness,
a config-based path that declares a model, tools, and instructions rather than wiring a graph
by hand, as the option to reach for "unless you have a specific reason to own the loop
yourself," which is itself a trade-off between speed and control rather than a claim that one
approach is simply better.
