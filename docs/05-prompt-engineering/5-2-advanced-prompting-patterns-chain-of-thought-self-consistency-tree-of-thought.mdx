## TL;DR

Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting (Wei et al., 2022) writes out intermediate reasoning steps in
few-shot examples instead of just final answers, substantially improving multi-step reasoning, an
effect that emerges mainly around 100 billion or more parameters (3.6) and can actively hurt smaller
models, which tend to produce fluent but illogical reasoning chains. Zero-shot CoT (Kojima et al.,
2022) shows that simply appending "Let's think step by step," with no worked examples at all, elicits
similar step-by-step reasoning and dramatically improves accuracy over a plain zero-shot prompt (5.1).
Self-consistency (Wang et al., 2022/2023) goes further: instead of committing to one greedily decoded
reasoning path, it samples several diverse paths via temperature or nucleus sampling (4.3) and takes a
majority vote over their final answers, discarding the reasoning text itself. Tree-of-thought (Yao et
al., 2023) generalizes this further still, maintaining a tree of intermediate reasoning steps, scoring
how promising each partial path looks, and using search with backtracking to abandon unpromising
branches, something neither chain-of-thought nor self-consistency ever do. Each technique adds
reliability at the cost of more inference calls, and none of them guarantees that a fluent-sounding
reasoning trace actually reflects how the model arrived at its answer (Turpin et al., 2023).

## High-Level Explanation

Picture four different ways to solve a hard word problem. Standard prompting is answering directly,
with no shown work. Chain-of-thought prompting is showing your work the way a math teacher asks: write
out each step that leads to the answer, which makes it easier to get multi-step problems right and
easier to catch a mistake along the way. Self-consistency is solving the same problem several times
independently, on separate scratch sheets, and then going with whichever final answer came up most
often, since a genuinely correct answer tends to be reached more consistently than a wrong one
arrived at by chance. Tree-of-thought is the most deliberate approach: sketching out several different
next steps on scratch paper, judging which ones look most promising, pursuing the best one further, and
being willing to cross out a branch entirely and back up to try a different approach if it stops
looking promising, rather than plowing ahead with the first idea that came to mind.

## Deep-Dive Explanation

**Chain-of-thought prompting, and why it mainly helps large models.** Wei et al.
([*Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11903),
2022) show that including a few few-shot exemplars whose reasoning steps are written out in full,
rather than jumping straight to the final answer, substantially improves accuracy on arithmetic,
commonsense, and symbolic reasoning benchmarks compared to standard answer-only few-shot prompting
(5.1). Critically, the paper reports that this benefit is scale-dependent: chain-of-thought prompting
"does not positively impact performance for small models, and only yields performance gains when used
with models of approximately 100 billion parameters." Below that scale, the paper finds models tend to
produce "fluent but illogical chains of thought," which can leave performance worse than standard
prompting, not merely unimproved. This is a specific instance of the emergent-ability pattern already
covered in 3.6: a capability that is close to absent below a scale threshold and substantially present
above it.

**Zero-shot chain-of-thought: no worked examples required.** Kojima et al.
([*Large Language Models are Zero-Shot Reasoners*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11916), 2022) show that
appending the single phrase "Let's think step by step" before the model's answer, with zero
chain-of-thought demonstrations in the prompt at all, reliably elicits similar step-by-step reasoning
and produces large accuracy gains over a plain zero-shot prompt, for instance raising accuracy on one
benchmark from roughly 18% to roughly 79% using the identical model. This means the reasoning benefit
chain-of-thought prompting provides does not strictly require worked examples; a simple instruction to
reason step by step can elicit much of the same behavior on its own, at a fraction of the prompt-design
effort.

**Self-consistency: sampling multiple paths and voting on the answer.** Wang et al.
([*Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11171),
2022, ICLR 2023) replace a single, greedily decoded chain-of-thought path with several independently
sampled reasoning paths, generated via temperature or nucleus sampling (4.3) rather than always
choosing the single highest-probability continuation, and then take a majority vote over the final
answers those paths reach, discarding the intermediate reasoning text itself. The intuition is that a
genuinely correct answer tends to be reachable by multiple different valid lines of reasoning, while an
incorrect answer is less likely to be reproduced consistently by chance across independently sampled
paths. This reliably improves accuracy over single-path chain-of-thought, at the direct cost of running
inference multiple times per question instead of once.

**Tree-of-thought: searching and backtracking across partial solutions.** Yao et al.
([*Tree of Thoughts: Deliberate Problem Solving with Large Language Models*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10601),
2023) generalize chain-of-thought and self-consistency further. Rather than committing to one linear
path or sampling several complete paths independently, the model maintains a tree of intermediate
reasoning steps, self-evaluates how promising each partial path looks so far, and uses a search
strategy such as breadth-first or depth-first search to decide which branches to continue exploring
and which to abandon, backtracking to try a different branch when a path stops looking promising. This
is the key capability chain-of-thought and self-consistency both lack: neither ever revisits or
abandons a reasoning path once started. On a constrained arithmetic puzzle task, this deliberate search
raised success from 4% under standard chain-of-thought prompting to 74%, though at substantially higher
inference cost, since evaluating and searching a tree of partial solutions requires many more model
calls than generating one or several complete paths.

**A related pattern: decomposing a problem instead of reasoning through it in one pass.**
Least-to-most prompting (Zhou et al.,
[*Least-to-Most Prompting Enables Complex Reasoning in Large Language Models*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10625),
2022) takes a structurally different approach: rather than reasoning through an entire problem in one
continuous pass, it first decomposes the problem into a sequence of simpler subproblems, then solves
them in order, feeding each subproblem's solution in as context for solving the next one. This proved
especially effective for problems requiring generalization to inputs harder than anything seen in the
prompt's examples, reaching over 99% accuracy on one compositional generalization benchmark where
standard chain-of-thought prompting reached only about 16%, since decomposition lets the model solve a
genuinely novel combination of familiar subproblems rather than needing the whole novel combination
demonstrated at once.

**A caution: a fluent reasoning trace is not proof of a genuine reasoning process.** Turpin et al.
([*Language Models Don't Always Say What They Think*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388), 2023) show
that chain-of-thought explanations can be systematically unfaithful to what actually drove a model's
answer. By deliberately biasing inputs, for instance always placing the correct multiple-choice answer
in the same position, they show a model's answer shifts to follow that bias while its stated reasoning
never mentions the biasing feature at all, instead constructing a plausible-sounding rationale for
whatever answer the bias produced. Accuracy dropped by as much as 36 percentage points across the
biased tasks studied. The practical implication is that a chain-of-thought trace can be a genuinely
useful tool for eliciting better answers while still being an unreliable explanation of why a
particular answer was reached, two different things that are easy to conflate.

**Choosing among these patterns is a cost-versus-reliability trade-off.** Zero-shot chain-of-thought
costs almost nothing extra beyond a single generation. Few-shot chain-of-thought costs a longer prompt
but still one generation. Self-consistency multiplies inference cost by however many paths are sampled,
in exchange for a more reliable final answer. Tree-of-thought multiplies cost further still, since it
requires generating, self-evaluating, and potentially backtracking across many partial paths, in
exchange for the ability to recover from a reasoning path that chain-of-thought or self-consistency
would have been stuck with. None of these techniques, including the more expensive ones, guarantees a
faithful account of the model's actual reasoning process, which matters most when the reasoning trace
itself, not just the final answer, is what a downstream process or reviewer intends to rely on.
