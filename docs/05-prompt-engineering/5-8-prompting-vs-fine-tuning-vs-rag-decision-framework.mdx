## TL;DR

Three different tools solve three different problems, and reaching for the wrong one is the most
common architectural mistake in a first LLM app. Prompting, writing clear instructions and a few
in-context examples, is the cheapest to try and should be tried first: OpenAI's own guidance states
plainly that "the prompt engineering process may be all you need in order to get great results for
your use case." Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) exists because a model's parametric knowledge
is frozen at training time; Lewis et al.'s original RAG paper (2020) names the core limitation
directly, that a pretrained model's "ability to access and precisely manipulate knowledge is still
limited," and that "providing provenance for their decisions and updating their world knowledge
remain open research problems." RAG solves exactly that: it grounds a response in retrieved,
current, citable documents. Fine-tuning solves a different problem entirely, baking a behavior,
format, or narrow skill into the model's weights so it no longer needs to be re-explained in every
prompt, at the cost of needing labeled training data, compute, and retraining whenever that behavior
needs to change. None of these three approaches is a strict replacement for either of the others, and
production systems routinely combine them, most notably fine-tuning a model specifically to make
better use of retrieved context, an approach the RAFT paper (Zhang et al., 2024) demonstrates
directly. This chapter is a decision framework for choosing and combining them; the mechanics of RAG
retrieval pipelines and fine-tuning techniques are covered in full in Phase 6 and Phase 7.

## High-Level Explanation

Picture training a new employee. Prompting is a one-page instruction sheet you hand them before
today's task, clear, specific, easy to revise tomorrow, but only as good as what fits on the page and
only in effect for as long as they're holding it. RAG is giving that same employee a reference binder
they can flip through mid-task, the binder can be updated overnight without retraining the employee
at all, and they can point to the exact page a fact came from. Fine-tuning is months of on-the-job
coaching until a behavior becomes second nature, no instruction sheet or binder required in the
moment, but every correction takes real time and effort, and it does not update itself when a policy
changes. A new task usually starts with the instruction sheet. It graduates to the binder when the
task needs facts that change or need a paper trail. It graduates to coaching when the same narrow
behavior has to happen thousands of times a day and re-explaining it in every instruction sheet has
become the expensive part.

## Deep-Dive Explanation

**Start with prompting, and mean it.** OpenAI's guidance on model optimization is direct about
sequencing: "the prompt engineering process may be all you need in order to get great results for
your use case." The same guidance names concrete benefits of fine-tuning "over prompting alone," but
frames them as reasons to graduate beyond prompting, not reasons to start elsewhere: "you can use
shorter prompts with fewer examples and context data, which saves on token costs at scale and can be
lower latency," and "you can train a smaller, cheaper, faster model to excel at a particular task,
where a larger model is not cost-effective."
Both of those benefits describe a cost problem that only exists once a prompt has already grown long
and a task is already running at high volume, not a reason to skip prompting on day one. The
practical discipline this implies: build an eval set first (the golden-dataset discipline from 5.6
applies here directly), see how far prompting actually gets you, and only then decide whether the gap
is a knowledge problem or a behavior problem.

**RAG exists because parametric memory has a cutoff and no paper trail.** Lewis et al.'s "Retrieval-
Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks" (2020) is the paper that named and formalized
this pattern. Its motivating claim is precise: large pretrained models' "ability to access and
precisely manipulate knowledge is still limited, and hence on knowledge-intensive tasks, their
performance lags behind task-specific architectures," and "providing provenance for their decisions
and updating their world knowledge remain open research problems." Their proposed architecture
combines "a pre-trained seq2seq model" as parametric memory with "a dense vector index of Wikipedia,
accessed with a pre-trained neural retriever" as non-parametric memory, retriever and generator
working together rather than the generator working alone. Anthropic's own framing of when this
matters is worth quoting directly: "RAG can be particularly useful for tasks that require up-to-date
information, domain-specific knowledge, or explicit citation of sources." A fine-tuned model cannot
offer any of those three properties on its own, no matter how well it was trained, because a fact
baked into weights during training has no timestamp, no source, and no way to update itself
overnight.

**Fine-tuning exists because some behaviors are expensive to keep re-explaining.** Anthropic's
glossary describes fine-tuning plainly: "the process of further training a pretrained language model
using additional data. This causes the model to start representing and mimicking the patterns and
characteristics of the fine-tuning dataset." OpenAI's supervised fine-tuning guidance names what it
is "best for": "Classification, Nuanced translation, Generating content in a specific format,
Correcting instruction-following failures." Full fine-tuning updates every weight in the model and is
expensive at real model scale; parameter-efficient techniques exist specifically to cut that cost.
LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) is the best known: it "freezes the pre-trained model weights and injects
trainable rank decomposition matrices into each layer of the Transformer architecture, greatly
reducing the number of trainable parameters for downstream tasks," reporting a reduction in trainable
parameters "by 10,000 times and the GPU memory requirement by 3 times" against full fine-tuning of a
175-billion-parameter model. This chapter does not go further into how LoRA or full fine-tuning
actually works; Phase 7 covers that in full. What matters here is only the shape of the tradeoff: real
upfront cost (labeled data, compute, an evaluation harness) in exchange for a model that no longer
needs the behavior re-explained in every request.

**A rough map from question to approach.** No single source states this as one settled formula, but
triangulating OpenAI, Anthropic, AWS, and Google Cloud's published guidance points at a consistent
shape. Google Cloud's own framing starts with exactly the right first question: "do you need the
model to always give a citation of a source grounded in your data? If yes, you will need to use RAG."
If not, the next question is about data volume and behavior complexity: "if you have only a small
amount of data, then prompt engineering may suffice." AWS's guidance frames the same RAG-versus-
fine-tuning choice around freshness and trust: "RAG can incorporate the latest documents in a few
minutes," while "fine-tuning can take a few hours to days, depending on the size of the model,"
and critically, "fine-tuned models do not provide a reference to the source in their responses,"
while "in its response, a RAG model provides a reference to the information source." Put the three
questions in sequence: does the answer need to cite current source material (RAG), does a small
number of well-chosen examples already demonstrate the desired behavior (prompting), and is this a
narrow, extremely high-volume task where the same behavior needs to happen without re-explaining it
every time (fine-tuning).

**The three approaches are not mutually exclusive.** Google Cloud's guidance says this outright: "you
can freely combine the aforementioned methods at your convenience." Snorkel AI's framing is more
direct still: "fine-tuning and RAG are not rivals, but complementary techniques that collaboratively
enhance LLMs." The clearest documented example of combining them is RAFT (Retrieval Augmented
Fine-Tuning, Zhang et al., 2024), "a training recipe that improves the model's ability to answer
questions in a 'open-book' in-domain settings" by training the model itself to tell a genuinely
relevant retrieved passage apart from an irrelevant distractor one, and to cite the passage it actually
used. A RAG system built on a model that was never trained to use retrieved context well can retrieve
a perfectly relevant document and still ignore it, or cite the wrong one; RAFT-style fine-tuning is
what closes that gap. A production system reaching real scale commonly ends up combining all three:
a fine-tuned model for consistent style and a narrow core skill, RAG for anything that needs to be
current or cited, and prompting for everything that varies request to request.
