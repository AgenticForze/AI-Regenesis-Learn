What Is Artificial Intelligence? History, AI Winters, Narrow vs. General AI

Chapter 1.1 · Phase 1

TL;DR​

Artificial intelligence is the field of building systems that do things we've historically assumed require human intelligence: recognizing a face, holding a conversation, deciding a next move. The odd thing about the label is that it keeps moving. Optical character recognition, chess engines, and spam filters were all once called "AI," and almost nobody calls them that today, because once a task is reliably automated it quietly gets reclassified as "just software" (sometimes called the AI effect). That's also why AI has a reputation for overpromising: twice in its history, funding and interest collapsed (two "AI winters") after the field's ambitions outran what the era's compute and data could support. The systems people mean when they say "AI" in 2026 are still narrow: extremely capable at specific tasks, not generally intelligent the way a human is. Think of AI's history less as one continuous invention and more like a relay race, where each runner hands off the baton once the previous approach hits its ceiling.

High-Level Explanation​

Six eras, each a response to the previous one's limits. The first wave (1950s to early 1970s) built symbolic, rule-based systems: hand-written logic that manipulated symbols according to explicit human-authored rules. It worked for tightly bounded problems (theorem proving, simple games) but broke down as soon as a problem's rule count grew combinatorially, a "combinatorial explosion" no amount of hand-authoring could keep up with. Funding followed enthusiasm, and when the approach hit its ceiling, funding collapsed: the first AI winter (roughly 1974–1980).

The second wave, expert systems (1980s), narrowed the ambition: instead of general reasoning, encode one domain expert's rules into a system that only had to be as good as that expert, in that one domain (medical diagnosis, equipment configuration). It found real commercial traction for a few years, then hit its own ceiling (brittle outside its narrow rule set, and costly to keep current as the world it modeled changed), triggering the second AI winter (late 1980s to early 1990s).

The thaw came from a different direction entirely: statistical machine learning (1990s–2010s) replaced hand-written rules with parameters learned from data. Deep learning (2012 onward) went further, learning the features themselves rather than requiring a human to engineer them. Generative AI, built on the 2017 Transformer architecture and mainstream since ChatGPT's November 2022 launch, learned to produce new content (text, images, code) rather than only classify or predict. Agentic AI (2023 onward) is the current frontier: systems that don't just generate an answer but decide a sequence of actions toward a goal, the subject of this site's flagship Phase 8. Both of these last two eras have earlier technical roots than their mainstream-adoption dates suggest; see the Deep-Dive's dating caveat below.

Layered on top of this timeline is a second, orthogonal distinction: narrow AI (ANI) versus general AI (AGI) versus the still-hypothetical superintelligence (ASI). This isn't about how "smart" a system seems in conversation. It's about generality: whether competence at one task transfers to unrelated tasks the way it does for a human. Every system across all six eras above, including today's most capable LLM agents, is narrow: remarkably capable within its training and tooling, but not able to walk into an unrelated cognitive task the way a person can.

Deep-Dive Explanation​

What actually caused the first AI winter. Two separate national retrenchments compound into what's remembered as a single winter. In the US, the 1966 ALPAC report, commissioned by the funders of machine-translation research, concluded MT wasn't living up to its promises; the 1969 Mansfield Amendment then redirected DARPA funding toward narrowly mission-relevant research, pulling money away from open-ended AI work through the mid-1970s. In the UK, the 1973 Lighthill Report, commissioned by the Science Research Council, concluded that AI research had failed to generalize past laboratory-scale toy problems and led directly to major UK funding cuts. These aren't one cause-and-effect chain; they are two governments independently reaching a similar conclusion within a few years of each other. The underlying technical problem was real, not just a funding story: symbolic search and rule systems scale combinatorially with problem size, and 1970s compute had no way to absorb that cost.

What ended it, and what caused the second winter. Expert systems narrowed the ambition enough to work commercially. MYCIN (Stanford, development began 1972) proved a rule-based system could match specialist-level diagnostic accuracy for antibiotic therapy in blinded evaluations, though it was never actually deployed at the bedside; liability and workflow-integration concerns kept it a research system rather than a commercial one. XCON (DEC's computer-configuration system, built at CMU and live in DEC production from 1980) is the one that proved the commercial case: by the mid-1980s it was processing tens of thousands of orders a year and saving DEC an estimated $25 million annually. That commercial success funded a boom in specialized Lisp-machine hardware companies. Most of that industry collapsed abruptly, in roughly a single year (1987), once general-purpose workstations and PCs matched Lisp machines on price and performance. Japan's Fifth Generation Computer Systems project is a separate story: it ran its full planned course from 1982 to 1994 and is judged a disappointment relative to its goals, not a company that folded alongside the Lisp-machine vendors. Expert systems more broadly also proved expensive to maintain, since every new edge case required a human expert to hand-author another rule. DARPA cut AI funding again in 1987.

Why deep learning "arrived" in 2012, not 1986. Backpropagation, the algorithm that makes training deep neural networks tractable, was popularized by Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams in 1986. It sat mostly dormant for 26 years, not because it was wrong, but because it needed two things the era didn't have: enough labeled data to avoid overfitting a network with millions of parameters, and enough parallel compute to train one in reasonable time. ImageNet supplied the first (introduced in 2009 with 3.2 million labeled images, later grown to roughly 14 million through continued crowdsourced annotation); GPUs, repurposed from graphics rendering, supplied the second. AlexNet's 2012 ImageNet win (a deep convolutional network with a 15.3% top-5 error rate, against roughly 26% for the next-best hand-engineered pipeline) is the conventional marker for when the field's center of gravity shifted to deep learning.

A dating caveat worth flagging. This chapter's era boundaries are conventional markers, not sharp cutoffs. Generative modeling predates the 2017 Transformer paper: GANs (2014) and VAEs (2013) were already producing synthetic images years earlier; what's specific to 2017 onward is the Transformer-based lineage that led to today's large language and diffusion models. Similarly, agentic AI's technical seed, the ReAct reasoning-and-acting pattern, was published in October 2022, several months before the 2023 mainstream tooling wave (autonomous-agent projects, widespread function-calling APIs) this chapter dates it to.

How narrowness is actually measured. Generality isn't asserted, it's tested via transfer: evaluate a system on tasks it wasn't specifically built for and see whether performance holds up without task-specific retraining. This is why agent evaluation work (Phase 15 covers this in depth) increasingly leans on out-of-distribution and multi-domain benchmarks rather than single-task leaderboards; a model that tops one narrow benchmark and collapses on a slightly different one is exhibiting exactly the task-specificity that defines narrow AI. A related mechanism worth naming: an LLM's apparent flexibility across topics in conversation is mostly in-context learning (using information provided in the prompt to shape a response) rather than weight-level generalization (the model's underlying parameters transferring competence the way learning does in a human). The two produce superficially similar fluency, but only one of them is what AGI would require.

Failure mode to watch for: conflating a narrow system's fluency with generality. A model that writes convincingly about a subject it has no grounded competence in (law, medicine, a codebase it has never seen) is exhibiting narrow linguistic capability, not general understanding. This distinction is the seed of the hallucination and over-trust failure modes covered throughout this site's Phase 8 and Phase 15.

Industry Use Cases​

Rule-based transaction fraud flags (expert-system era)

Business problem

Manual review of every transaction was too slow to catch fraud before settlement.

Solution sketch

Hand-authored if-then rule engines flagged transactions matching known fraud patterns in real time.

Watch for

Rules go stale as fraud patterns evolve, and are easily reverse-engineered once a fraud ring learns the thresholds.

Statistical ML credit scoring

Business problem

Manual loan underwriting was slow and inconsistent across underwriters.

Solution sketch

Logistic-regression and scorecard models trained on historical repayment data standardized decisions.

Watch for

Historical repayment data can bake in proxy discrimination that regulators (fair lending law) specifically look for.

Deep learning check and document image processing

Business problem

Manually keying data from scanned checks and forms was slow and error-prone.

Solution sketch

CNN-based image models read handwritten and printed fields directly from scanned documents.

Watch for

Silent accuracy drops on document formats or handwriting styles underrepresented in training data.

Agentic research and filings-review assistant

Business problem

Analysts spend hours summarizing filings and cross-referencing prior research.

Solution sketch

An LLM agent reads source documents, drafts a cited summary memo, and flags discrepancies for review.

Watch for

The agent misattributes or fabricates a filing detail in the summary memo, and it reaches a decision-maker without the analyst re-checking primary sources: a control gap under model-risk-management frameworks like SR 11-7.
