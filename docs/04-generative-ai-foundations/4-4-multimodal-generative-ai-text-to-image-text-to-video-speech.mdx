## TL;DR

The model families from 4.2 and the decoding mechanisms from 4.3 are not tied to text alone; this
chapter follows them into images, video, and speech. **Text-to-image** generation conditions a
diffusion model (4.2) on a text prompt using cross-attention to a text encoder's embedding (such as
CLIP's or a large language model's), strengthened at sampling time by **classifier-free guidance**, a
technique that sharpens prompt adherence without needing a separate classifier. **Text-to-video**
extends the same diffusion mechanism across a temporal dimension in addition to space, the added
complexity being frame-to-frame consistency, which one prominent architecture addresses by treating
video as spacetime patches processed by a diffusion transformer rather than by generating independent
frames. **Text-to-speech**, by contrast, frequently abandons diffusion for the **autoregressive,
token-based mechanism** from 3.3 and 4.1: a neural audio codec first compresses raw audio into
discrete tokens, and a language model then generates new audio one token at a time, conditioned on
text and, often, a short reference clip of a target voice. Which mechanism wins is not fixed by
modality; it follows the same trade-offs the generative learning trilemma (4.1) already established.

## High-Level Explanation

Picture three different specialists given the same request: describe a rainy city street at night.
An illustrator given that description paints a single still image. A filmmaker given the same
description shoots a few seconds of moving footage, which is a harder job than the illustrator's
because every frame has to agree with the frames around it, a car cannot teleport six feet between
one frame and the next. A voice actor given a written script reads it aloud in a natural-sounding
voice, which is a different kind of problem again: getting the rhythm, tone, and pronunciation right
across time, much closer to how a person reads a sentence one word at a time than to painting a
picture. The interesting result is that the illustrator and the filmmaker usually reach for the same
underlying technique, painting from noise (diffusion, 4.2), adapted for the filmmaker's extra
time dimension, while the voice actor's job turns out to resemble the autoregressive, one-piece-at-a-
time mechanism a text-generating LLM already uses, just applied to chunks of sound instead of words.

## Deep-Dive Explanation

**Conditioning a diffusion model on text.** The diffusion mechanism from 4.2 by itself only knows how
to reverse noise into "a plausible image from the training distribution," with no way to steer which
image. Text conditioning adds cross-attention (2.5, 3.3) inside the denoising network: at every
denoising step, the network attends over an embedding of the text prompt, not just over the noisy
image itself, letting the prompt steer each step of the reverse process. That text embedding usually
comes from a separately pretrained text encoder. CLIP (Radford et al.,
[*Learning Transferable Visual Models From Natural Language Supervision*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020),
2021) trains an image encoder and a text encoder together via a contrastive objective so that matching
image-text pairs land close together in a shared embedding space, giving diffusion models a
ready-made text embedding aligned with visual content. Imagen (Saharia et al.,
[*Photorealistic Text-to-Image Diffusion Models with Deep Language Understanding*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11487),
2022) instead uses a large, frozen general-purpose language model's text encoder (T5), and finds that
scaling up the text encoder specifically improves prompt fidelity more than scaling up the
image-diffusion component does, evidence that language understanding, not just image quality, is a
binding constraint on faithful text-to-image generation.

**Classifier-free guidance.** An earlier technique for steering diffusion toward a condition used a
separately trained classifier to nudge the sampling process toward images that classifier scored as
matching the prompt. Classifier-free guidance (Ho and Salimans,
[*Classifier-Free Diffusion Guidance*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.12598), 2022) removes the separate
classifier entirely: the same diffusion model is trained both with the text conditioning present and,
some fraction of the time, with it randomly dropped, so one model learns both a conditional and an
unconditional version of the denoising step. At sampling time, the model runs both versions at each
step and extrapolates away from the unconditional prediction and toward the conditional one by a
tunable guidance strength, which sharpens prompt adherence considerably. This technique is now
standard in essentially all deployed text-to-image and text-to-video diffusion systems, not an
optional extra.

**Extending diffusion to video: the added complication is time.** A video is not simply a stack of
independently generated images; if each frame were diffused independently, objects would flicker,
jitter, or teleport between frames because nothing enforces agreement across time. OpenAI's technical
report on its Sora video model, [*Video generation models as world
simulators*](https://openai.com/index/video-generation-models-as-world-simulators/) (2024), describes
one architectural approach to this problem: representing video as a collection of spacetime patches,
small chunks of the data that span both a spatial region and a span of time together, rather than
patches of a single still frame, and processing that representation with a diffusion transformer
(a transformer-based denoiser, an architectural variant of the diffusion mechanism from 4.2) instead
of a convolutional U-Net. This lets the same explicit-but-approximate-density diffusion mechanism
operate over a video's full spatiotemporal volume directly, rather than generating frames
independently and patching temporal consistency on afterward. This is one documented architectural
pattern for text-to-video generation, not the only possible one, and the specific systems available
change quickly; the underlying principle, that the temporal dimension has to be modeled directly
rather than bolted on, is the durable part.

**Speech generation: borrowing the autoregressive mechanism instead of diffusion.** Unlike image and
video generation, a substantial share of modern text-to-speech systems do not use diffusion as their
primary generative mechanism; they instead reuse the autoregressive, explicit-density mechanism from
3.3, 4.1, and 4.2, applied to audio. A neural audio codec first compresses raw audio into a sequence
of discrete tokens, conceptually similar to how BPE, WordPiece, or SentencePiece (3.5) tokenize text,
except learned specifically to compress and reconstruct audio rather than text. VALL-E (Wang et al.,
[*Neural Codec Language Models are Zero-Shot Text to Speech Synthesizers*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02111),
2023) then trains a language model to autoregressively predict a sequence of these audio tokens,
conditioned on the input text and a short reference clip of a target voice, generating new speech in
that voice one audio token at a time, the same causal, exact-tractable-density mechanism (3.3, 4.1)
an LLM uses to generate text tokens, just applied to a different, learned vocabulary.

**Mechanism choice still follows the trilemma, not the modality.** Image and video generation lean
toward diffusion because sample quality and diversity outweigh sampling-speed cost for those
modalities (4.1's trilemma, 4.2). Speech generation often leans toward the autoregressive route
instead, because speech is inherently sequential in the way text is, and a well-trained audio-token
language model can capture natural prosody and voice characteristics token by token without paying
diffusion's characteristic sampling-speed cost. The lesson is not "images use diffusion, audio uses
autoregression" as a fixed rule, but that the same trade-offs from 4.1 and 4.2 continue to determine
the right mechanism, per modality and per task, rather than the modality dictating the mechanism by
itself.
