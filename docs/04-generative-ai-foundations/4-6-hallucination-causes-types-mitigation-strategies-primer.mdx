## TL;DR

**Hallucination** is an LLM generating content that is false or unsupported, stated with the same
fluent confidence as accurate output. Ji et al. split it into **intrinsic hallucination** (the output
directly contradicts a given source) and **extrinsic hallucination** (the output cannot be verified
from the source at all, whether or not it happens to be true). Hallucination is not simply a bug to
be patched away: Kalai and Vempala show that even a well-calibrated model trained on entirely accurate
data must hallucinate at some baseline rate on facts that appeared rarely in training, and a 2025
OpenAI paper argues hallucination persists in deployed systems partly because standard evaluation
scores a wrong, confident answer no worse than an honest "I don't know," rewarding guessing over
calibrated abstention. Exposure bias from training (3.3) and randomness from sampling-based decoding
(4.3) further compound the base rate. Detection methods like SelfCheckGPT and semantic entropy catch
likely hallucinations after the fact by sampling multiple outputs; mitigation techniques like
Chain-of-Verification and retrieval-augmented generation, previewed here and covered fully in Phase 6,
intervene earlier, at generation time or by grounding the model in external evidence.

## High-Level Explanation

Picture a multiple-choice exam scored the ordinary way: one point for a correct answer, zero for a
wrong one, and zero for leaving a question blank. A student who is unsure of an answer is still better
off guessing than leaving it blank, since a guess sometimes pays off and a blank never does, even
though the guess is really just confident-sounding uncertainty. An LLM trained and evaluated under a
similar scoring pattern faces the same incentive: producing a specific, fluent, plausible-sounding
answer scores better on average than expressing uncertainty or declining to answer, even when the
model's actual grasp of the fact in question is closer to a guess than to genuine knowledge. Nothing
in the model's output signals which case it is: a confidently stated but hallucinated fact sounds
exactly like a confidently stated and correct one.

## Deep-Dive Explanation

**Defining hallucination, and its two-way split.** Ji et al.
([*Survey of Hallucination in Natural Language Generation*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.03629),
2022/2023) define hallucination as generated content that is nonsensical or unfaithful to an intended
source, and split it into two kinds. **Intrinsic hallucination** directly contradicts the source
content provided, for example a summary that states a policy took effect in 2021 when the source
document says 2019. **Extrinsic hallucination** is content that the source can neither confirm nor
deny, information the model added that may or may not be true but simply is not present in, or
derivable from, what it was given. Both count as hallucination even though only intrinsic
hallucination is directly checkable against the source text in front of the model.

**Hallucination has a statistical floor, not just a training-quality problem.** Kalai and Vempala
([*Calibrated Language Models Must Hallucinate*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.14648), 2023, STOC 2024)
prove a lower bound: even a model trained on entirely accurate data and perfectly calibrated to that
data's statistics must still hallucinate at some baseline rate on facts that appeared only once or a
handful of times in training, roughly tied to how many such singleton facts exist. This result applies
most strongly to arbitrary, rare facts with no learnable pattern behind them, such as a specific
person's exact birthdate, and applies far less to systematic knowledge like arithmetic, where the
underlying structure is learnable rather than memorized fact by fact. The practical implication is
that some hallucination on long-tail facts is not a defect that better training data alone eliminates;
it is close to a mathematical floor for that class of fact.

**Why hallucination persists in deployed systems: an evaluation-incentive problem.** A 2025 paper from
Kalai, Nachum, Vempala, and Zhang
([*Why Language Models Hallucinate*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664), 2025) extends the exam analogy
from the High-Level section into a direct argument: standard benchmark scoring is binary, right or
wrong, and treats a wrong confident answer identically to an honest expression of uncertainty, scoring
both as zero. Under that incentive, a model optimized to perform well on such benchmarks is statistically
pushed toward guessing confidently rather than abstaining, the same way a test-taker is pushed toward
guessing on a multiple-choice question with no penalty for a wrong answer. The paper's proposed fix is
not another hallucination-specific benchmark, but changing how existing benchmarks are scored, so that
calibrated abstention is rewarded rather than penalized as harshly as a confident wrong answer.

**Compounding factors: exposure bias, sampling, and the long tail.** Several mechanics already covered
in this curriculum add to the baseline rate above. Teacher forcing during training creates exposure
bias (3.3), a mismatch where the model never practices recovering from its own earlier mistakes, since
training always feeds it the correct prior token regardless of what it would have generated itself.
Sampling-based decoding strategies (4.3) such as temperature scaling and nucleus sampling deliberately
introduce randomness to avoid repetitive output, which also means an unlucky sample can occasionally
drift toward a less-supported continuation, particularly at higher temperature. And Kandpal et al.
([*Large Language Models Struggle to Learn Long-Tail Knowledge*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.08411),
2023) show that factual accuracy correlates strongly with how often a fact appeared in pretraining
data, meaning hallucination risk is not evenly distributed: it concentrates on rare entities and
niche facts specifically, not on well-represented, frequently repeated ones.

**Detecting likely hallucination without a ground-truth source.** Two black-box techniques catch
likely hallucination by exploiting a single insight: sample the same prompt multiple times and check
whether the answers agree. SelfCheckGPT (Manakul, Liusie, and Gales,
[*SelfCheckGPT: Zero-Resource Black-Box Hallucination Detection for Generative Large Language
Models*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08896), 2023) generates several independent responses to the same
prompt and checks their mutual consistency, on the reasoning that a model drawing on a genuinely known
fact tends to reproduce it consistently across samples, while a hallucinated detail is more likely to
vary from sample to sample, since there is no real fact anchoring it. Semantic entropy (Farquhar et
al., [*Detecting hallucinations in large language models using semantic entropy*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07421-0),
Nature, 2024) refines this by clustering sampled outputs according to whether they mean the same
thing, not whether they are worded the same way, and then measuring entropy over those meaning-based
clusters rather than over raw token sequences; high semantic entropy, many meaningfully different
answers to the same question, flags likely confabulation. Neither method requires access to model
internals or an external knowledge base, only the ability to sample the model repeatedly.

**Mitigating hallucination: verification and grounding.** Chain-of-Verification (Dhuliawala et al.,
[*Chain-of-Verification Reduces Hallucination in Large Language Models*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.11495),
2023) has the model draft an initial answer, independently generate verification questions targeting
specific claims within that draft, answer those verification questions on their own without reference
to the original draft's framing, and then revise the original answer based on any inconsistencies the
verification step surfaces. This catches errors the model can identify when prompted to check its own
claims individually, even though it produced those same claims fluently the first time. Retrieval-augmented
generation (Lewis et al.,
[*Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401),
2020), previewed here and covered fully in Phase 6, takes a different approach: rather than relying
solely on facts memorized during training, a retriever fetches relevant external documents at
inference time and the model conditions its generation on them, shrinking the gap between what the
model must recall from memory and what it can read directly from a grounded source.

**No single technique eliminates hallucination.** The statistical floor from Kalai and Vempala means
some baseline rate on rare, singleton facts is unlikely to reach zero regardless of technique.
Detection methods like SelfCheckGPT and semantic entropy flag likely hallucination after generation
but do not prevent it. Chain-of-Verification catches some errors the model can self-identify when
prompted correctly, but is not exhaustive. Retrieval reduces reliance on memorized facts for content
the retriever actually surfaces, but does not help for reasoning or synthesis the retrieved documents
do not directly answer. In practice, a production system combines several of these techniques rather
than treating any single one as sufficient on its own.
