## TL;DR

Chapter 4.1 split generative models into explicit-density and implicit-density, and named the
generative learning trilemma: no family gets high sample quality, full data diversity, and fast
sampling all at once. This chapter puts four concrete families on that map. **Autoregressive LLMs**
(3.3) compute an exact, tractable probability one token at a time, the same explicit-density
mechanism whether the output is text or, less commonly, pixels. **Generative Adversarial Networks
(GANs)** are implicit-density: a generator and a discriminator train against each other, producing
fast, sharp samples at the cost of the mode-collapse risk covered in 4.1. **Variational Autoencoders
(VAEs)** are explicit-density too, but only approximately: an encoder compresses input into a
compact latent distribution and a decoder reconstructs from it, trained by maximizing a lower bound
on the likelihood rather than the exact likelihood itself. **Diffusion models** learn to reverse a
gradual noising process step by step, and as of 2026 are the dominant family for high-quality image,
video, and audio generation, because their combination of quality and diversity outweighs their
sampling-speed cost for those modalities, especially once fast-sampling techniques like distillation
are applied.

## High-Level Explanation

Picture four different ways an art student could learn to produce a new painting after studying a
gallery of originals. The autoregressive approach paints one brushstroke at a time, each stroke
chosen from a genuine probability distribution over what could come next given every stroke so far,
the way an LLM chooses one token at a time. The GAN approach is two students working against each
other: a forger who paints quickly and a critic who tries to catch fakes, with the forger improving
only by fooling the critic, never by any direct feedback about the original gallery. The VAE approach
first compresses every painting down to a short, structured description, then learns to expand any
plausible description back into a full painting, so new paintings come from picking a new
description and expanding it. The diffusion approach starts from pure static and slowly, over many
small steps, cleans the static up into a painting, having only ever practiced by adding static to
real paintings and learning to undo exactly that.

## Deep-Dive Explanation

**Autoregressive models, extended beyond text.** The causal, one-token-at-a-time mechanism from 3.3
is not inherently text-specific; PixelRNN and PixelCNN (van den Oord et al., 2016) applied the same
autoregressive factorization to individual pixels, predicting each pixel's value from every pixel
generated before it in a fixed scan order. This confirms the mechanism generalizes, but it also shows
its cost: an image has vastly more elements than a sentence has tokens, so a strictly pixel-by-pixel
autoregressive image model is far slower to sample from than a comparably capable diffusion model,
which is why autoregressive generation remains the default for text but not for images.

**VAEs: explicit density, but only approximately.** A VAE's encoder does not map an input to a single
latent vector; it maps the input to the parameters of a distribution (typically a mean and variance)
over a compact latent space, and a specific latent vector is then sampled from that distribution. The
decoder reconstructs an output from that sampled latent. Training maximizes the evidence lower bound
(ELBO), which combines a reconstruction term (the decoder should recover the original input from its
latent code) with a term that pulls the encoder's output distribution toward a simple prior, usually
a standard normal distribution, so that new samples can later be produced by drawing from that same
prior and decoding. Unlike an autoregressive model's exact tractable likelihood, the ELBO is only a
lower bound on the true data likelihood (Kingma and Welling, [*Auto-Encoding Variational
Bayes*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6114), 2013), which is why VAEs are explicit-density in spirit but
only approximately so in practice, a middle position between an autoregressive model's exact density
and a GAN's complete absence of one. In exchange, a well-trained VAE's latent space tends to be
smooth and structured enough to interpolate between two samples meaningfully, a property GANs and
raw diffusion models do not automatically provide.

**GANs, revisited mechanically.** 4.1 introduced GANs as the standard implicit-density example;
Goodfellow et al.'s original formulation ([*Generative Adversarial Networks*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2661),
2014) frames training as a minimax game: the generator tries to maximize the discriminator's error
rate, the discriminator tries to correctly classify real versus generated samples, and at the
game's theoretical equilibrium the generator's output distribution matches the real data
distribution exactly. In practice, this equilibrium is difficult to reach reliably, which is the
deeper reason GANs are prone to mode collapse (4.1) and to unstable training dynamics that later GAN
variants (not covered here) attempt to address.

**Diffusion models: reversing a known corruption process.** A diffusion model defines a forward
process that gradually adds a small amount of Gaussian noise to a real training sample over many
steps (commonly on the order of a thousand), until the sample is indistinguishable from pure noise.
Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (Ho, Jain, and Abbeel,
[*Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11239), 2020) show that a
neural network can be trained to reverse this process one small step at a time, predicting and
removing a bit of noise at each step, so that starting from pure random noise and repeatedly applying
the trained reverse step produces a new, realistic sample. Like a VAE, this training objective is a
variational bound rather than an exact likelihood, making diffusion models explicit-but-approximate
density as well; the key practical difference from a VAE is that a diffusion model reaches high
quality and diversity by spreading the generation task across many small, individually easy steps
rather than one large one, which is precisely the slow-sampling cost the generative learning
trilemma (4.1) describes. Running the full noising-and-denoising process directly on raw pixels is
expensive; Latent Diffusion Models (Rombach et al.,
[*High-Resolution Image Synthesis with Latent Diffusion Models*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752),
2022, the architecture behind Stable Diffusion) instead run the diffusion process inside a much
smaller, VAE-compressed latent space and only decode back to pixels at the end, combining a VAE's
compression with a diffusion model's denoising to make high-resolution image generation practical.
Techniques such as Consistency Models (Song et al.,
[*Consistency Models*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01469), 2023) further address diffusion's slow
sampling directly, training a model to map a noisy input to a clean sample in a small handful of
steps, or even one, trading a small amount of quality for a large reduction in sampling cost. This is
the trilemma being actively pushed on rather than resolved: as of 2026, diffusion-family models
remain the dominant choice for image, video, and audio generation precisely because techniques like
these have narrowed their speed disadvantage without giving up their quality and diversity advantage
over GANs.
