## TL;DR

Before deep learning, and still very much in production today, a handful of classical algorithm
families cover most tabular machine learning problems: linear models, decision trees, ensembles of
trees, k-nearest neighbors, support vector machines, and Naive Bayes. None of them is universally
"best." Each makes a different structural assumption about how inputs map to outputs, called an
inductive bias, and that assumption is what determines whether it fits a given problem well, how
interpretable it is, and where it sits on chapter 1.4's bias-variance spectrum. Choosing among them
is a practical decision driven by data size, feature types, interpretability requirements, and how
much non-linearity the true relationship actually has.

## High-Level Explanation

Every algorithm family answers the same question differently: given a feature vector (chapter
1.4), what shape of function connects it to the output, and how flexible is that shape allowed to
be? That answer is the algorithm's inductive bias.

**Linear and logistic regression** assume the relationship is a straight line (regression) or a
straight decision boundary (logistic regression, for classification): the output is a weighted sum
of the input features. This is a strong, restrictive assumption, which is exactly what makes these
models fast, stable, and easy to explain: every prediction can be traced to a specific weighted
contribution from each feature.

**Decision trees** make almost the opposite assumption: they repeatedly ask a yes/no question about
one feature at a time (is income above $50,000?) and split the data accordingly, building a
flowchart of splits that ends in a prediction. This lets a tree represent non-linear relationships
and mixed feature types without any manual transformation, but a tree grown deep enough to fit its
training data closely is exactly the high-variance, overfitting case from chapter 1.4: a small
change in the training data can produce a very different tree.

**Ensembles** combine many individual models, usually trees, to get a stronger predictor than any
one of them alone. Bagging (bootstrap aggregating) trains many trees independently on randomly
resampled subsets of the data and averages their predictions, which cancels out each tree's
individual variance; Random Forest is bagging plus one more trick, randomizing which features each
split is even allowed to consider. Boosting instead builds trees one at a time, each new tree
specifically trained to correct the errors the ensemble so far is still making, which drives down
bias rather than variance.

**k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN)** makes no assumption about a functional shape at all: to predict a new
point, it finds the k most similar points already seen and predicts based on their labels. It has
no real training phase (all the work happens at prediction time), which makes it simple to reason
about but slow to query and, as covered in the Deep-Dive, unreliable once the number of features
grows large.

**Support vector machines (SVM) and Naive Bayes** round out the classical landscape. SVMs find the
decision boundary that maximizes the margin, the distance to the nearest points of each class, and
can be extended to non-linear boundaries via the kernel trick. Naive Bayes applies Bayes' theorem
under a simplifying assumption that features are independent given the class, which is rarely
literally true but is often good enough, especially on high-dimensional, sparse data.

## Deep-Dive Explanation

**Linear and logistic regression, formally.** Linear regression predicts ŷ = w·x + b, a weighted
sum of the features plus a bias term, fit by minimizing the sum of squared residuals between ŷ and
the true y, either in closed form (the normal equation) or by gradient descent for large datasets.
Logistic regression predicts a probability by passing that same linear combination through the
sigmoid function, P(y=1 | x) = 1 / (1 + e^-(w·x + b)), and is fit by maximizing the likelihood of
the observed labels (equivalently, minimizing log loss). Both are high-bias, low-variance models in
chapter 1.4's terms: the linear form cannot represent a genuinely curved relationship no matter how
much data it sees, but it is very stable across different training samples.

**Decision trees, formally.** The CART algorithm (Breiman, Friedman, Olshen, and Stone,
[*Classification and Regression Trees*](https://www.routledge.com/Classification-and-Regression-Trees/Breiman-Friedman-Stone-Olshen/p/book/9780412048418),
1984) builds a tree by recursively choosing, at each node, the single feature and threshold that
splits the data into the two purest possible child groups, where purity is measured by Gini
impurity or information gain (entropy reduction). Splitting continues until a stopping condition is
reached: a maximum depth, a minimum number of samples per leaf, or no split that improves purity.
Those stopping conditions are hyperparameters in chapter 1.4's sense, and they directly control the
tree's position on the bias-variance spectrum: an unconstrained tree grown to purity is low bias
and high variance (it can represent almost any relationship, including the training set's noise),
while a shallow, heavily constrained tree shifts back toward high bias.

**Bagging and boosting, formally.** Bagging trains B independent trees, each on a bootstrap sample
(a random sample of the same size as the original dataset, drawn with replacement) and, for
classification, predicts by majority vote across the B trees. Random Forest (Breiman,
[*Random Forests*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1010933404324),
*Machine Learning*, 2001) adds one refinement: at each split, only a random subset of features is
even considered, which decorrelates the individual trees further and improves how much variance the
averaging step actually cancels. Gradient boosting (Friedman,
[*Greedy Function Approximation: A Gradient Boosting Machine*](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/annals-of-statistics/volume-29/issue-5/Greedy-function-approximation-A-gradient-boosting-machine/10.1214/aos/1013203451.full),
*Annals of Statistics*, 2001) instead trains trees sequentially, where each new tree is fit to the
ensemble's current errors, formally the negative gradient of the loss function, which is exactly
the residual in the common squared-error regression case and a close analogue of it for
classification losses; AdaBoost (Freund and Schapire,
[*A Decision-Theoretic Generalization of On-Line Learning and an Application to Boosting*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S002200009791504X),
*Journal of Computer and System Sciences*, 1997) is an earlier boosting variant that instead
reweights misclassified examples more heavily before training the next tree. Both boosting
variants reduce bias: each additional tree specifically targets whatever the ensemble is still
getting wrong, so the ensemble as a whole can represent relationships no single shallow tree could.
The practical tradeoff mirrors chapter 1.4 directly: bagging is the variance-reduction tool,
boosting is the bias-reduction tool, and the two are frequently combined (a gradient-boosted
ensemble of intentionally shallow trees).

**k-Nearest Neighbors, formally.** For a query point x, kNN computes the distance (commonly
Euclidean) from x to every point in the training set, takes the k closest, and predicts the
majority class (classification) or the average value (regression) among them (Cover and Hart,
[*Nearest Neighbor Pattern Classification*](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/1053964),
*IEEE Transactions on Information Theory*, 1967). It is called a lazy learner because no model is
fit in advance; all the computation is deferred to prediction time, which becomes expensive as the
training set grows. A smaller k produces a more flexible, higher-variance boundary (sensitive to
individual noisy points); a larger k smooths the boundary toward higher bias. kNN also degrades in
a specific way as the number of features grows, sometimes called the curse of dimensionality: in
high-dimensional space, the volume between points grows so fast that the distance to the nearest
neighbor and the distance to a typical randomly chosen point converge, so "nearest" stops being a
meaningfully informative relationship (Bellman,
[*Dynamic Programming*](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691146683/dynamic-programming),
Princeton University Press, 1957, coined the term in a different context; its specific consequence
for distance-based methods is a widely documented empirical property of high-dimensional spaces
rather than a single-paper result).

**Support vector machines and Naive Bayes, briefly.** An SVM (Cortes and Vapnik,
[*Support-Vector Networks*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00994018),
*Machine Learning*, 1995) finds the linear boundary that maximizes the margin, the distance from
the boundary to the nearest point of either class, which tends to generalize better than an
arbitrary linear separator. The kernel trick extends this to non-linear boundaries by implicitly
mapping features into a higher-dimensional space where a linear separator corresponds to a curved
boundary in the original space, without ever computing that mapping explicitly. Naive Bayes applies
Bayes' theorem, P(class | features) is proportional to P(features | class) times P(class), under the
simplifying assumption that every feature is conditionally independent of every other feature given
the class. That assumption is almost always literally false (a patient's symptoms are correlated
with each other, not independent), but the resulting probability estimates are frequently still
useful for ranking or thresholding decisions even when the underlying probabilities are miscalibrated,
which is why the algorithm remains a common, cheap baseline for high-dimensional, sparse data like
text.
