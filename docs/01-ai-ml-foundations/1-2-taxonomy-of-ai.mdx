## TL;DR

The five terms from 1.1's timeline (rule-based AI, machine learning, deep learning, generative AI,
agentic AI; folding 1.1's separate rule-based and expert-systems eras into one rule-based layer,
and its statistical-ML era into machine learning) aren't five competing technologies. They're five layers, each a narrower
specialization of the one below it: rule-based systems run any human-authored logic, machine
learning narrows that to logic learned from data, deep learning narrows further to logic learned
without hand-built features, generative AI narrows further still to systems that produce new
content rather than a label, and agentic AI narrows the most by choosing its own sequence of
actions. It's similar to medical specialization: a general practitioner can treat a wide range of
conditions; a subspecialist treats a narrower set, but goes deeper on each one. A real production
system today usually stacks several of these layers together rather than picking just one.

## High-Level Explanation

Picture the five layers as a pyramid, widest at the base. **Rule-based AI** is the widest layer:
any system whose logic a person wrote out explicitly, like an if/then rule flagging a suspicious
transaction. **Machine learning** narrows that down to systems whose logic was learned from data
instead of hand-written, like a logistic-regression scorecard fit to historical repayment records.
**Deep learning** narrows further to learning systems that also learn their own features rather
than relying on a human to engineer them, like a CNN that learns to recognize handwriting directly
from pixels. **Generative AI** narrows further to systems that produce new content rather than a
label or score, like a model that writes a paragraph or draws an image. **Agentic AI**, at the
narrow tip, adds one more constraint on top of generative capability: the system chooses its own
sequence of actions toward a goal rather than producing one output and stopping.

![Five layers of the AI taxonomy, narrowing from rule-based systems at the base to agentic AI at the tip, each layer a specialization of the one beneath it](/img/illustrations/1-2-layered-stack.svg)

The narrowing matters because it tracks a real tradeoff. A wider layer is more general and more
predictable; a narrower layer is more powerful within its scope but also more specific about what
it can do and how it was built. None of the layers make the ones below them obsolete: a rule-based
input check still runs in front of a machine-learned model in most production systems, for the
simple reason that catching an obviously malformed input with one line of code is cheaper and more
reliable than asking a trained model to notice it.

## Deep-Dive Explanation

**What formally distinguishes each layer.** The distinction isn't about how sophisticated a system
looks; it's about where its decision logic came from.

- *Rule-based*: the decision procedure is fully specified by a human author, as explicit branching
  logic. Nothing is learned; the same input always produces the same output because a person chose
  every rule. In practice these are usually implemented as production-rule engines that either
  match rules forward from known facts to a conclusion (forward chaining, the pattern MYCIN used)
  or work backward from a candidate conclusion to the facts needed to prove it (backward chaining).
  Because every interaction between rules has to be anticipated by the author, the rule set's
  complexity grows combinatorially as more rules are added. That maintenance burden is the
  underlying cause of the expert-systems maintenance problem covered in 1.1.
- *Machine learning*: a loss function defines what "good" means for the task, and an optimization
  procedure, gradient descent for many contemporary parametric models, greedy recursive splitting
  for a decision tree, or a closed-form solution for methods like ordinary least squares, fits a
  set of parameters to minimize that loss over training data. The logic is learned, but a human
  still chooses the features the model sees.
- *Deep learning*: a composition of many parameterized layers learns its own intermediate
  representations directly from raw or lightly processed input, removing the manual
  feature-engineering step. The learning procedure, backpropagation to compute how the loss
  changes with respect to each parameter, then gradient descent to update the parameters using
  that computation, is the same gradient-based idea as other machine learning, just applied to a
  much deeper, more expressive function.
- *Generative AI*: the model samples new content (a full sentence, image, or other artifact) from
  a learned probability distribution over outputs, most often one conditioned on an input and
  written P(output | input). This is a narrower, modern-usage version of an older statistical
  distinction: a *discriminative* model predicts P(label | input) directly to draw a boundary
  between fixed classes (an SVM or logistic-regression credit-approval model, for example), while
  a classical *generative* model instead learns the joint distribution of inputs and labels,
  P(input, label), and can sample new inputs from it (a Naive Bayes spam filter is the standard
  textbook example of this classical sense). What the AI industry calls "generative AI" inherits
  that second model's ability to sample new examples, but almost always in the conditional
  P(output | input) form rather than the unconditional or joint form the classical term describes.
  Autoregressive language models make the bridge between those two forms concrete: the model is
  trained on the full joint probability of a token sequence, factored one token at a time as
  P(x₁, x₂, ..., xₜ) = P(x₁) · P(x₂ | x₁) · P(x₃ | x₁, x₂) · ... · P(xₜ | x₁, ..., xₜ₋₁). Prompting
  the model is what turns that joint factorization into the conditional case: the prompt fixes the
  first several tokens, and generation only samples the conditionals for the tokens that come
  after them.
- *Agentic*: a control loop wraps the model, so the system perceives its current state, decides on
  a next action, executes it, and observes the result before deciding again (see **Agent** in the
  [glossary](../00-orientation/0-5-glossary-notation-conventions.md#core-terms)). What changes here
  isn't the underlying model. It's that the model's output now selects an action inside a loop
  instead of being the final answer. Phase 8 (8.1–8.3) covers this loop in full. This chapter's
  pyramid stacks agentic AI on top of generative AI because that is today's dominant pattern (an
  LLM's generated text becomes the action to take), but the control-loop idea itself is older and
  more general: a rule-based thermostat or a reinforcement-learning agent built on a non-generative
  policy network is also agentic in the perceive/decide/act sense, with no generative layer
  underneath. Reinforcement learning, covered in depth in [chapter 1.3](./1-3-ml-foundations.mdx),
  is a related but separate idea worth disentangling from this one: it's an optimization technique
  that can show up inside several of these layers rather than being a layer of its own. A Q-table
  is tabular machine learning; a deep Q-network is deep learning; RLHF fine-tunes a generative
  model using a reward signal; and some agentic systems use RL to shape which action their policy
  selects. Which layer a system belongs to still depends on where its logic came from, not on
  whether RL was involved in producing it.

**Where the boundary blurs.** A decision tree is a useful edge case: its parameters (which
feature to split on, at what threshold, at every node) are learned from data by a greedy
algorithm, which formally makes it machine learning. But the resulting decision procedure, once
trained, is a sequence of if/then branches that a person could read, print, and follow by hand,
indistinguishable in *form* from a rule system a human wrote directly (see the classic CART
formulation in Breiman et al.,
[*Classification and Regression Trees*](https://www.routledge.com/Classification-and-Regression-Trees/Breiman-Friedman-Stone-Olshen/p/book/9780412048418),
1984). This is the taxonomy's actual point: the layers classify a system by *how its logic was
produced*, not by what the logic looks like afterward, and a system can straddle the line.

**A common misclassification worth naming.** A model built with deep learning that outputs a
fixed label, such as a fine-tuned transformer that classifies a support ticket into one of ten
categories, is not generative AI. It's a deep-learning discriminative model: it draws a boundary
between ten known classes rather than modeling a distribution it can sample new content from. Deep
learning and generative AI overlap heavily in today's systems (most generative models are also
deep learning models) but the two properties are independent, and the taxonomy layer a system
belongs to depends on which property is being asked about.
