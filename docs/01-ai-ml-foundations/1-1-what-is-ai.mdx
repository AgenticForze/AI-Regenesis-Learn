## TL;DR

Artificial intelligence is the field of building systems that do things we've historically
assumed require human intelligence: recognizing a face, holding a conversation, deciding a next
move. The odd thing about the label is that it keeps moving — optical character recognition,
chess engines, and spam filters were all once called "AI," and almost nobody calls them that
today, because once a task is reliably automated it quietly gets reclassified as "just software."
That's also why AI has a reputation for overpromising: twice in its history, funding and interest
collapsed (two "AI winters") after the field's ambitions outran what the era's compute and data
could support. The systems people mean when they say "AI" in 2026 are still **narrow** —
extremely capable at specific tasks, not generally intelligent the way a human is. Think of AI's
history less as one continuous invention and more like a relay race, where each runner
(rule-based systems, expert systems, statistical ML, deep learning, generative AI, agentic AI)
hands off the baton once the previous approach hits its ceiling.

## High-Level Explanation

Six eras, each a response to the previous one's limits. The first wave (1950s–early 1970s) built
**symbolic, rule-based systems**: hand-written logic that manipulated symbols according to
explicit human-authored rules. It worked for tightly bounded problems (theorem proving, simple
games) but broke down as soon as a problem's rule count grew combinatorially — a "combinatorial
explosion" no amount of hand-authoring could keep up with. Funding followed enthusiasm, and when
the approach hit its ceiling, funding collapsed: the **first AI winter** (roughly 1974–1980).

The second wave, **expert systems** (1980s), narrowed the ambition: instead of general reasoning,
encode one domain expert's rules into a system that only had to be as good as that expert, in that
one domain (medical diagnosis, equipment configuration). It found real commercial traction for a
few years, then hit its own ceiling — expert systems were brittle outside their narrow rule set
and expensive to maintain — triggering the **second AI winter** (late 1980s–early 1990s).

The thaw came from a different direction entirely: **statistical machine learning** (1990s–2010s)
replaced hand-written rules with parameters *learned* from data. **Deep learning** (2012 onward)
went further, learning the features themselves rather than requiring a human to engineer them.
**Generative AI** (2017 onward, accelerating sharply after 2022) learned to produce new content —
text, images, code — rather than only classify or predict. **Agentic AI** (2023 onward) is the
current frontier: systems that don't just generate an answer but decide a *sequence* of actions
toward a goal, the subject of this site's flagship Phase 8.

![Six eras of AI history, from rule-based systems through agentic AI, with the two AI winters marked as dips in funding and interest between eras](/img/illustrations/1-1-ai-timeline.svg)

Layered on top of this timeline is a second, orthogonal distinction: **narrow AI (ANI)** versus
**general AI (AGI)** versus the still-hypothetical **superintelligence (ASI)**. This isn't about
how "smart" a system seems in conversation — it's about *generality*: whether competence at one
task transfers to unrelated tasks the way it does for a human. Every system across all six eras
above, including today's most capable LLM agents, is narrow: remarkably capable within its
training and tooling, but not able to walk into an unrelated cognitive task the way a person can.

## Deep-Dive Explanation

**What actually caused the first AI winter.** The UK's 1973 Lighthill Report, commissioned by the
Science Research Council, concluded that AI research had failed to deliver on its promises and
that most results didn't generalize past laboratory-scale toy problems. It directly led to major
UK funding cuts, and DARPA in the US followed with its own retrenchment after early machine
translation efforts (formally, the 1966 ALPAC report) failed to produce usable results. The
underlying technical problem was real, not just a PR failure: symbolic search and rule systems
scale combinatorially with problem size, and 1970s compute had no way to absorb that cost.

**What ended it, and what caused the second winter.** Expert systems like MYCIN (antibiotic
therapy recommendations, Stanford, early 1970s) and XCON (DEC's computer-configuration system,
commercially deployed from 1980) proved narrow rule-based AI could pay for itself. That success
funded a boom — specialized Lisp-machine hardware companies, Japan's Fifth Generation Computer
Systems project — that collapsed for two compounding reasons: general-purpose workstations (Sun,
and later cheap PCs) matched Lisp machines on price/performance by the late 1980s, and expert
systems proved expensive to maintain because every new edge case required a human expert to
hand-author another rule. DARPA cut funding again in 1987.

**Why deep learning "arrived" in 2012, not 1986.** Backpropagation — the algorithm that makes
training deep neural networks tractable — was popularized by Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams in
1986. It sat mostly dormant for 26 years not because it was wrong, but because it needed two
things the era didn't have: enough labeled data to avoid overfitting a network with millions of
parameters, and enough parallel compute to train one in reasonable time. ImageNet (released 2009,
~14 million labeled images) supplied the first; GPUs, repurposed from graphics rendering, supplied
the second. AlexNet's 2012 ImageNet win — a deep convolutional network beating every prior
hand-engineered computer-vision pipeline by a wide margin — is the conventional marker for when
the field's center of gravity shifted to deep learning.

**Narrow AI, formally.** A system is narrow to the extent that its learned competence is
task-specific: a model trained to play chess doesn't thereby know anything about protein folding,
even if both are "hard" problems by human standards. AGI would require competence that transfers
across essentially arbitrary cognitive tasks, the way human general intelligence does; no system
built in any of the six eras above has demonstrated this. ASI is a hypothetical further step —
general intelligence that substantially exceeds human capability across the board — and remains
outside the scope of what any current system does; treat claims of AGI or ASI arrival with the
same evidentiary standard as any other extraordinary technical claim, and check the date on any
source making one, since this is an actively and publicly contested question as of 2026.

**Failure mode to watch for:** conflating a narrow system's fluency with generality. A model that
writes convincingly about a subject it has no grounded competence in (law, medicine, a codebase it
has never seen) is exhibiting narrow linguistic capability, not general understanding — this
distinction is the seed of the hallucination and over-trust failure modes covered throughout this
site's Phase 8 and Phase 15.
