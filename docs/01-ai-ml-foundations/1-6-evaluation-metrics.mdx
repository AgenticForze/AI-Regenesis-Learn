## TL;DR

A model's performance is not one number, it is several, and picking the wrong one hides real
failures. Accuracy is the most intuitive metric and the easiest to get badly wrong, because it
falls apart on the imbalanced data that shows up constantly in fraud, disease screening, and
churn. Precision and recall separate two different kinds of mistakes, false alarms and missed
cases, and F1 balances them into one number when both matter about equally. ROC-AUC summarizes
how well a model ranks positives above negatives across every possible decision threshold. RMSE
plays a similar role for regression, where the target is a number rather than a class. Choosing a
metric is choosing what kind of error the business actually cares about most.

## High-Level Explanation

Every classifier's predictions on a labeled test set can be sorted into a confusion matrix: cases
the model correctly flagged as positive (true positives), cases it correctly flagged as negative
(true negatives), cases it wrongly flagged as positive (false positives, false alarms), and cases
it wrongly flagged as negative (false negatives, misses). Every classification metric in this
chapter is built from just those four counts.

**Accuracy**, the fraction of predictions that were correct overall, is the most natural metric to
reach for, and the most dangerous on skewed data. If 99% of transactions are legitimate, a model
that predicts "legitimate" for everything scores 99% accuracy while catching zero fraud. Accuracy
treats every correct prediction as equally valuable, which is exactly wrong when one class is rare
and the cost of missing it is high.

**Precision and recall** split the failure modes apart. Precision asks: of everything the model
flagged as positive, how much actually was? Recall asks: of everything that actually was positive,
how much did the model catch? A model can push either one toward 1.0 trivially: flag nothing to
get perfect precision (on the cases it does flag), or flag everything to get perfect recall. Real
systems trade one against the other, and which one to prioritize is a business decision, not a
modeling one: missing a fraudulent transaction and blocking a legitimate one are not equally
costly.

**F1** is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, a single number that stays low unless both
are reasonably high. It is the standard default when neither false positives nor false negatives
are clearly more expensive.

**ROC-AUC** takes a step back from any single decision threshold. Instead of asking "is this
particular flag right or wrong," it asks how well the model's raw scores rank actual positives
above actual negatives across every threshold at once, summarized as a single area-under-curve
number between 0.5 (no better than random) and 1.0 (perfect ranking).

**RMSE** answers the equivalent question for regression, where the target is a continuous number
like a price or a demand forecast rather than a class. It measures the typical size of the gap
between predicted and actual values, in the same units as the target, with large errors penalized
more heavily than small ones.

## Deep-Dive Explanation

**The confusion matrix, formally.** For a binary classifier with a fixed decision threshold, let TP,
FP, FN, and TN be the counts of true positives, false positives, false negatives, and true
negatives on the evaluation set. Every metric below is a ratio of these four counts, or of ratios
computed across a sweep of thresholds.

**Accuracy, precision, recall, and specificity.** Accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN).
Precision = TP / (TP + FP): of the positive predictions, the fraction that were correct. Recall,
also called sensitivity or the true positive rate, = TP / (TP + FN): of the actual positives, the
fraction the model found. Specificity, the true negative rate, = TN / (TN + FP): of the actual
negatives, the fraction correctly rejected. Precision and recall are computed over different
denominators (predicted-positive count versus actual-positive count), which is exactly why a model
can score high on one and low on the other.

**F1 and the F-measure family.** F1 = 2 · (Precision · Recall) / (Precision + Recall), the harmonic
mean rather than the arithmetic mean, which matters: the harmonic mean is pulled down sharply by
whichever of the two is smaller, so a model cannot compensate for very low recall with very high
precision and still score a high F1. This formulation, generalized to weight precision and recall
unequally (the Fβ family), was formalized by van Rijsbergen in the information retrieval literature
([*Information Retrieval*](https://openlib.org/home/krichel/courses/lis618/readings/rijsbergen79_infor_retriev.pdf),
2nd edition, Butterworths, 1979). For multi-class problems, precision, recall, and F1 are computed
per class and then averaged, either as a macro-average (unweighted mean across classes, which
treats rare and common classes equally) or a weighted average (weighted by how often each class
appears, which matches the class distribution of the data).

**ROC curves and AUC, formally.** A classifier that outputs a score or probability, not just a
hard label, produces a different confusion matrix at every possible decision threshold. The ROC
(receiver operating characteristic) curve plots the true positive rate against the false positive
rate (FPR = FP / (FP + TN)) as the threshold sweeps from one extreme to the other. The area under
that curve (AUC) has a clean probabilistic interpretation: it equals the probability that a
randomly chosen actual positive receives a higher score than a randomly chosen actual negative
(Hanley and McNeil,
[*The Meaning and Use of the Area Under a Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Curve*](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/10.1148/radiology.143.1.7063747),
*Radiology*, 1982). An AUC of 0.5 means the model ranks positives and negatives no better than a
coin flip; 1.0 means every actual positive outranks every actual negative. AUC's main limitation is
that it can look reassuringly high on strongly imbalanced data even when the model is nearly
useless at the operating threshold anyone would actually deploy, because the metric averages
performance across thresholds few practitioners would ever use. The precision-recall curve, which
plots precision against recall across the same threshold sweep, is the standard alternative when
the positive class is rare, because unlike the ROC curve it is sensitive to how well the model
does specifically on the minority class (Davis and Goadrich,
[*The Relationship Between Precision-Recall and ROC Curves*](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1143844.1143874),
*ICML*, 2006).

**RMSE, MAE, and R², formally.** For a regression model with predictions ŷᵢ and true values yᵢ
over n examples, the root mean squared error is RMSE = √((1/n) · Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²): squaring each
residual before averaging means large errors contribute disproportionately more than small ones,
so RMSE is sensitive to outliers. The mean absolute error, MAE = (1/n) · Σ|yᵢ − ŷᵢ|, weighs every
error in proportion to its size and is more robust to a few large misses, which is why RMSE and MAE
can disagree about which of two models is better: a model with many small errors and one huge
outlier can have a lower MAE but higher RMSE than a model with consistent, moderate errors. R²
(the coefficient of determination) reframes error as 1 − (sum of squared residuals) / (total sum
of squared deviations from the mean), interpretable as the fraction of the target's variance the
model explains; an R² of 0 means the model does no better than always predicting the mean, and a
negative R² means it does worse than that trivial baseline.
