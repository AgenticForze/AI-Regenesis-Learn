## TL;DR

Chapter 7.3 covered teaching a model to behave the way people want. This chapter covers a different
kind of adaptation: giving a model knowledge it did not have, whether that means a specialized
vocabulary and writing style (domain adaptation) or simply keeping a model current as the world moves
on (continual pretraining). Gururangan et al. showed that "a second phase of pretraining in-domain
(domain-adaptive pretraining) leads to performance gains, under both high- and low-resource settings,"
and that "adapting to the task's unlabeled data (task-adaptive pretraining) improves performance even
after domain-adaptive pretraining." Any further training carries a cost, though: catastrophic
forgetting. Kirkpatrick et al. found that "it has been widely thought that catastrophic forgetting is
an inevitable feature of connectionist models," and a 2023 study of large language models specifically
found something genuinely counterintuitive: "as the model scale increases, the severity of forgetting
intensifies." Two well-established levers push back against that. Kirkpatrick's Elastic Weight
Consolidation works by "selectively slowing down learning on the weights important for" earlier tasks.
More recent continual-pretraining research found a simpler recipe also works: "a simple and scalable
combination of learning rate (LR) re-warming, LR re-decaying, and replay of previous data is sufficient
to match the performance of fully re-training from scratch on all available data." This chapter covers
when to reach for domain adaptation instead of full continual pretraining, why forgetting happens, and
how to structure a training run that keeps it in check.

## High-Level Explanation

Getting new knowledge into a model sits on a spectrum, and where a team lands on it depends on how much
knowledge needs to change and how permanent that change should be. At one end, retrieval and prompting
(Chapters 5.8, 6.1) add facts at inference time without touching a single weight: fast, reversible,
cheap. Fine-tuning (Chapters 7.1, 7.2) changes weights, but on a narrow, labeled task. Domain-adaptive
pretraining (DAPT) is a step further still: instead of a labeled task, it continues the model's original
self-supervised objective, next-token prediction, on unlabeled text from a target domain, teaching the
model that domain's vocabulary and style without teaching it any specific task. Task-adaptive
pretraining (TAPT) narrows that same idea to the unlabeled text of the task itself. Full continual
pretraining goes furthest: it repeats this process over a model's entire lifetime, folding in new data
as the world changes, so the model's knowledge does not freeze at its original training cutoff. Each
step further along this spectrum reaches deeper into what the model actually knows, and each step
further along it also carries more risk of the model quietly losing what it already knew.

## Deep-Dive Explanation

**Domain-adaptive and task-adaptive pretraining extend the original pretraining objective onto new
text, no labels required, and Gururangan et al. showed the gains from doing this are real and stack.**
Their study spanned "four domains (biomedical and computer science publications, news, and reviews) and
eight classification tasks," and found that this "second phase of pretraining in-domain
(domain-adaptive pretraining) leads to performance gains, under both high- and low-resource settings."
Critically, DAPT and TAPT are not substitutes for each other: "adapting to the task's unlabeled data
(task-adaptive pretraining) improves performance even after domain-adaptive pretraining," meaning a
team that has already run DAPT still has more to gain from narrowing further to TAPT. Where domain data
is scarce or unavailable, the paper found a workable fallback: "adapting to a task corpus augmented
using simple data selection strategies is an effective alternative." The overall conclusion was
consistent across every setting tested: "we consistently find that multi-phase adaptive pretraining
offers large gains in task performance."

**Every one of these further training passes risks catastrophic forgetting, and the mechanism behind it
has been understood since long before large language models existed.** Kirkpatrick et al. opened their
paper on Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) with the core problem: "it has been widely thought that
catastrophic forgetting is an inevitable feature of connectionist models," because a network trained
sequentially on a new task will overwrite the weights an earlier task depended on. EWC's fix is to make
some weights harder to change than others: the method works "by selectively slowing down learning on
the weights important for" tasks the model has already learned, identifying which weights matter using
each weight's estimated importance to prior performance, then penalizing further changes to the most
important ones during later training. This is not just a small-model, small-task result. A 2023 study
of large language models under continual instruction tuning reported something genuinely
counterintuitive: "the experiments reveal that catastrophic forgetting is generally observed in LLMs
ranging from 1b to 7b parameters," and, more surprisingly, "as the model scale increases, the severity
of forgetting intensifies," which the authors attribute to larger models having "much significant
initial performance" that later training has more room to erode. The same study found the choice of
architecture and preparation matters too: a decoder-only model retained more of its prior knowledge
than a comparable encoder-decoder model, and models that had already been through general instruction
tuning forgot less during later, narrower fine-tuning than models that had not.

**Continual pretraining, repeatedly updating a model's full knowledge base over its lifetime rather
than adapting it once to one domain, needs a recipe that avoids both catastrophic forgetting and the
cost of retraining from scratch every time.** Ibrahim et al. tested this directly and found a
comparatively simple approach was enough: "a simple and scalable combination of learning rate (LR)
re-warming, LR re-decaying, and replay of previous data is sufficient to match the performance of fully
re-training from scratch on all available data." Each piece addresses a specific failure mode.
Re-warming the learning rate when new data arrives lets the model actually absorb it, since a learning
rate that has already decayed toward zero from the original training run barely moves the weights at
all. Re-decaying it afterward stabilizes training on the combined old-and-new distribution the same way
the original pretraining run stabilized on its own data. Replay, mixing a sampled fraction of the
original pretraining data back in alongside the new data, directly counters forgetting by continuing to
show the model examples of what it already knew, rather than training on new data exclusively. The team
also found the standard cosine learning rate schedule itself was part of the problem at update time,
since it assumes a fixed, known token budget set in advance, and proposed "alternatives to the cosine
learning rate schedule that help circumvent forgetting induced by LR re-warming and that are not bound
to a fixed token budget," making the approach usable for a model that will be updated again and again
rather than trained once and left alone.

## Hands-On: Measure Catastrophic Forgetting with a Replay Buffer

The clearest way to see catastrophic forgetting is to measure it directly: fine-tune a model on a narrow
domain, then check how much worse it got at something it used to do well, with and without replaying a
slice of the original data.

**Step 1: Set up a base model and two text sources.** One is the narrow domain being adapted to; the
other is a small general-purpose sample used only to measure forgetting, never trained on directly in
the no-replay condition.

```python
import copy
import torch
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

torch.manual_seed(0)  # keeps the two fine-tuning runs comparable across repeated executions

model_name = "distilgpt2"
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(model_name)
tokenizer.pad_token = tokenizer.eos_token
base_model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(model_name)

domain_texts = [
    "The claimant's policy excludes coverage for pre-existing conditions disclosed at binding.",
    "Under the indemnity clause, the insurer's liability is capped at the sum insured on the schedule.",
    "Subrogation rights transfer to the insurer upon settlement of the claim in full.",
]
general_texts = [
    "The cat sat by the window and watched the rain fall on the quiet street.",
    "She packed her bag for the trip and double-checked the tickets one more time.",
    "The bakery down the road sells fresh bread every morning before the rush.",
]
```

**Step 2: Write a perplexity function to measure how well a model predicts held-out general text.**
Rising perplexity on `general_texts` after fine-tuning is the forgetting signal this experiment is built
to catch.

```python
def average_perplexity(model, tokenizer, texts):
    model.eval()
    losses = []
    with torch.no_grad():
        for text in texts:
            input_ids = tokenizer(text, return_tensors="pt").input_ids
            loss = model(input_ids, labels=input_ids).loss
            losses.append(loss.item())
    mean_loss = sum(losses) / len(losses)
    return torch.exp(torch.tensor(mean_loss)).item()

baseline_ppl = average_perplexity(base_model, tokenizer, general_texts)
print(f"baseline perplexity on general text: {baseline_ppl:.2f}")
```

**Step 3: Fine-tune one copy on domain text only, no replay.** This is the condition expected to forget
the most.

```python
def fine_tune(model, tokenizer, texts, steps=30, lr=5e-5):
    model.train()
    optimizer = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=lr)
    for step in range(steps):
        text = texts[step % len(texts)]
        input_ids = tokenizer(text, return_tensors="pt").input_ids
        loss = model(input_ids, labels=input_ids).loss
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()
        optimizer.zero_grad()
    return model

no_replay_model = copy.deepcopy(base_model)
no_replay_model = fine_tune(no_replay_model, tokenizer, domain_texts, steps=30)
no_replay_ppl = average_perplexity(no_replay_model, tokenizer, general_texts)
print(f"perplexity after fine-tuning, no replay: {no_replay_ppl:.2f}")
```

**Step 4: Fine-tune a second copy the same way, but mix a replayed slice of general text into every few
steps.** Nothing else about training changes, only whether the model keeps seeing a sample of what it
already knew.

```python
def fine_tune_with_replay(model, tokenizer, domain_texts, replay_texts, steps=30, lr=5e-5, replay_every=3):
    model.train()
    optimizer = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=lr)
    for step in range(steps):
        use_replay = (step % replay_every == 0)
        text = replay_texts[step % len(replay_texts)] if use_replay else domain_texts[step % len(domain_texts)]
        input_ids = tokenizer(text, return_tensors="pt").input_ids
        loss = model(input_ids, labels=input_ids).loss
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()
        optimizer.zero_grad()
    return model

replay_model = copy.deepcopy(base_model)
replay_model = fine_tune_with_replay(replay_model, tokenizer, domain_texts, general_texts, steps=30, replay_every=3)
replay_ppl = average_perplexity(replay_model, tokenizer, general_texts)
print(f"perplexity after fine-tuning, with replay: {replay_ppl:.2f}")

print(f"forgetting without replay: {no_replay_ppl - baseline_ppl:+.2f} perplexity")
print(f"forgetting with replay:    {replay_ppl - baseline_ppl:+.2f} perplexity")
```

With this toy setup, only three domain sentences and thirty steps, the effect is exaggerated compared to
a real training run, but the direction is the same one Ibrahim et al. reported at production scale: the
no-replay model's perplexity on `general_texts` rises noticeably, while the replay model's rises by
much less, because it never stopped seeing examples of the distribution it started with.

Domain adaptation and continual pretraining are not alternatives to task fine-tuning, they are stages
that can precede it. A model that has been through DAPT and TAPT still goes through the same fine-tuning
step covered in Chapter 7.2; it just starts that step already fluent in the domain instead of learning
the domain and the task at the same time.
