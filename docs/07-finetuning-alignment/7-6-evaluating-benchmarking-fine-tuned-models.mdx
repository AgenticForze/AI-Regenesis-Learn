## TL;DR

Every technique in this phase, fine-tuning, alignment, domain adaptation, compression, changes a model.
This chapter covers how to tell whether a change was actually an improvement. Three checks matter, and
none of them alone is sufficient. General-capability benchmarks catch regressions the fine-tuning task
itself would never reveal: MMLU "covers 57 tasks including elementary mathematics, US history, computer
science, law, and more," precisely so a team can check whether a model still knows things the fine-tuning
data never touched. Open-ended quality, the kind a multiple-choice benchmark cannot score, usually needs
an LLM-as-judge or a human rater: Zheng et al. found that "strong LLM judges like GPT-4 can match both
controlled and crowdsourced human preferences well, achieving over 80% agreement, the same level of
agreement between humans," though they caution that such judges carry "position, verbosity, and
self-enhancement biases, as well as limited reasoning ability." And every benchmark number needs a
contamination check before it is trusted at all: one study found that "in the MMLU benchmark, ChatGPT
and GPT-4 demonstrated an exact match rate of 52% and 57%, respectively, in guessing the missing
options," a level of accuracy consistent with the benchmark having leaked into training data rather than
genuine reasoning. A fine-tuned model that passes its own task, scores well on a general benchmark, and
earns good marks from a judge or a human, still has not proven any of those checks were measuring
something real, unless contamination was ruled out first.

## High-Level Explanation

Evaluating a fine-tuned model well means checking three different things, because a single number from a
single test cannot answer all three questions at once. First: did the fine-tune actually improve the
narrow behavior it targeted? That needs a held-out set built from the same distribution as the
fine-tuning task, examples the model never trained on, scored against the task's own success criteria.
Second: did the fine-tune cost the model anything outside that narrow behavior? That is the catastrophic
forgetting question from Chapter 7.4, and it needs a broad, general-capability check run before and after
the fine-tune, not just a single post-hoc score. Third: for output that is open-ended rather than
multiple-choice or exact-match, prose, code, a conversation turn, how good is good? Automatic metrics
struggle here, which is why LLM-as-judge and human evaluation exist, each with its own failure modes to
account for. A fourth concern cuts across all three: any benchmark score, task-specific or general, is
only meaningful if the benchmark itself was not part of what the model trained on, directly or
indirectly. Skipping that check does not just risk one bad number, it can make every other check in the
pipeline look more convincing than it actually is.

## Deep-Dive Explanation

**General-capability benchmarks exist to catch regressions a narrow fine-tuning task would never surface
on its own, and MMLU is the standard reference point for that check.** Hendrycks et al. built the
benchmark specifically to probe breadth: "we propose a new test to measure a text model's multitask
accuracy. The test covers 57 tasks including elementary mathematics, US history, computer science, law,
and more." Performing well requires "extensive world knowledge and problem solving ability" across
subjects that have nothing to do with any one fine-tuning task, which is exactly what makes it useful as
a before-and-after regression check: a fine-tuned model's MMLU score dropping relative to its base
model's score is a direct, general signal that something outside the fine-tuning task got worse, the
same signal Chapter 7.4 discussed in the specific context of catastrophic forgetting. The original paper
also reported how far even strong models were from mastering the benchmark: the largest GPT-3 model
tested "improves over random chance by almost 20 percentage points on average," yet "on every one of the
57 tasks, the best models still need substantial improvements before they can reach expert-level
accuracy," and models generally, GPT-3 included in the paper's per-subject results, "still have
near-random accuracy on some socially important subjects such as morality and law." A benchmark this far
from saturated is a genuinely useful regression signal; a benchmark models already ace tells a team much
less about whether a specific fine-tune made things worse.

**Open-ended output, the kind neither multiple-choice nor exact-match scoring can grade, usually needs a
judge, and Zheng et al.'s study of using a strong LLM as that judge found it works surprisingly well,
with real caveats.** The core problem they set out to solve was that "evaluating large language model
(LLM) based chat assistants is challenging due to their broad capabilities and the inadequacy of existing
benchmarks in measuring human preferences." Testing GPT-4 as a judge against both a controlled benchmark
and a crowdsourced platform, they found "strong LLM judges like GPT-4 can match both controlled and
crowdsourced human preferences well, achieving over 80% agreement, the same level of agreement between
humans," meaning an LLM judge and a human rater disagree about as often as two human raters disagree with
each other. That result comes with real limitations, though: the same study documented "position,
verbosity, and self-enhancement biases, as well as limited reasoning ability" in LLM judges, meaning
which answer is shown first, how long an answer is, and whether the judge model is scoring its own family
of outputs can all skew a result independent of actual quality. None of that makes LLM-as-judge useless;
it makes it a tool whose known biases need to be actively controlled for, randomizing answer order and
tracking judge-model identity separately from the models being judged, rather than trusted at face value.

**No benchmark number, general or task-specific, means anything until contamination has been ruled out,
and modern benchmarks are demonstrably not immune.** A study built specifically to probe this found a
striking result: "in the MMLU benchmark, ChatGPT and GPT-4 demonstrated an exact match rate of 52% and
57%, respectively, in guessing the missing options," meaning that when the correct answer choice was
hidden from a question and the model was asked to guess what was missing, it succeeded at a rate far
above what genuine reasoning over an unfamiliar question would predict, evidence consistent with the
benchmark's exact text having appeared somewhere in training data. The study's framing of why this
matters is direct: "recent observations have underscored a disparity between the inflated benchmark
scores and the actual performance of LLMs, raising concerns about potential contamination of evaluation
benchmarks." For a team fine-tuning a model, this risk is not just about the base model's original
pretraining, fine-tuning datasets themselves can accidentally include benchmark questions or
near-duplicates scraped from the same public sources the benchmark was built from, inflating a
post-fine-tune score in a way that has nothing to do with the fine-tune actually working.

## Hands-On: Build a Regression Eval Harness

The clearest way to catch a fine-tune's hidden cost is to measure general capability before and after,
on questions that have nothing to do with what was fine-tuned. This builds a small multiple-choice
harness in the same style MMLU-style benchmarks use, and runs it against a model before and after a
narrow fine-tune.

**Step 1: Define a small general-knowledge eval set, unrelated to the fine-tuning domain.** Each item has
a question, four labeled choices, and a correct letter, the same shape MMLU questions use.

```python
eval_set = [
    {"question": "The chemical symbol for water is", "choices": {"A": "H2O", "B": "CO2", "C": "NaCl", "D": "O2"}, "answer": "A"},
    {"question": "The largest planet in the solar system is", "choices": {"A": "Earth", "B": "Jupiter", "C": "Mars", "D": "Venus"}, "answer": "B"},
    {"question": "The author of Romeo and Juliet is", "choices": {"A": "Dickens", "B": "Shakespeare", "C": "Austen", "D": "Twain"}, "answer": "B"},
    {"question": "The capital of Japan is", "choices": {"A": "Seoul", "B": "Beijing", "C": "Tokyo", "D": "Bangkok"}, "answer": "C"},
    {"question": "The freezing point of water in Celsius is", "choices": {"A": "0", "B": "32", "C": "100", "D": "-10"}, "answer": "A"},
    {"question": "The gas plants absorb for photosynthesis is", "choices": {"A": "Oxygen", "B": "Nitrogen", "C": "Carbon dioxide", "D": "Hydrogen"}, "answer": "C"},
]
```

**Step 2: Score each answer choice by the model's log-probability of its letter, the standard
zero-shot multiple-choice scoring method.** The model never generates free text here; it only needs to
rank four single tokens.

```python
import torch
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

model_name = "distilgpt2"
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(model_name)
base_model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(model_name)
base_model.eval()

def score_choices(model, tokenizer, item):
    prompt = item["question"] + "\n"
    for letter, text in item["choices"].items():
        prompt += f"{letter}. {text}\n"
    prompt += "Answer:"
    input_ids = tokenizer(prompt, return_tensors="pt").input_ids
    with torch.no_grad():
        logits = model(input_ids).logits
    next_token_logits = logits[0, -1, :]
    scores = {}
    for letter in item["choices"]:
        token_id = tokenizer.encode(" " + letter, add_special_tokens=False)[0]
        scores[letter] = next_token_logits[token_id].item()
    return max(scores, key=scores.get)

def accuracy(model, tokenizer, eval_set):
    correct = sum(score_choices(model, tokenizer, item) == item["answer"] for item in eval_set)
    return correct / len(eval_set)

base_accuracy = accuracy(base_model, tokenizer, eval_set)
print(f"base model regression-eval accuracy: {base_accuracy:.2f}")
```

**Step 3: Fine-tune a copy of the model on a narrow domain, the same no-replay setup from Chapter
7.4, then run the identical eval set against it.** Nothing about the eval set changes; only the model
does.

```python
import copy

domain_texts = [
    "The claimant's policy excludes coverage for pre-existing conditions disclosed at binding.",
    "Under the indemnity clause, the insurer's liability is capped at the sum insured on the schedule.",
    "Subrogation rights transfer to the insurer upon settlement of the claim in full.",
]

def fine_tune(model, tokenizer, texts, steps=30, lr=5e-5):
    model.train()
    optimizer = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=lr)
    for step in range(steps):
        text = texts[step % len(texts)]
        input_ids = tokenizer(text, return_tensors="pt").input_ids
        loss = model(input_ids, labels=input_ids).loss
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()
        optimizer.zero_grad()
    model.eval()
    return model

fine_tuned_model = copy.deepcopy(base_model)
fine_tuned_model = fine_tune(fine_tuned_model, tokenizer, domain_texts, steps=30)

fine_tuned_accuracy = accuracy(fine_tuned_model, tokenizer, eval_set)
print(f"fine-tuned model regression-eval accuracy: {fine_tuned_accuracy:.2f}")
print(f"change: {(fine_tuned_accuracy - base_accuracy) * 100:+.1f} percentage points")
```

**Step 4: Read the result as a regression signal, not a capability score.** With only six questions and
a tiny demo model, the absolute accuracy numbers here are not meaningful on their own, a real
general-capability check needs a benchmark the size of MMLU to say anything reliable. What this harness
demonstrates is the pattern: the exact same eval set, run against the model before and after fine-tuning,
turns catastrophic forgetting from an abstract risk into a concrete before-and-after number a team can
put a threshold on before shipping.

A fine-tuned model that fails the contamination check has not really passed any of the other checks
either, even if their raw scores look good; the scores just are not evidence of anything until a clean
evaluation set confirms they mean what they appear to mean.
