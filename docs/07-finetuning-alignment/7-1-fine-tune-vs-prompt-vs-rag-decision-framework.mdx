## TL;DR

Chapter 5.8 gave the first version of this decision: prompting first, RAG for a knowledge problem,
fine-tuning for a behavior problem. This chapter revisits it with more weight behind it, now that Phase
6 covered exactly how RAG works and Phase 7 is about to cover exactly how fine-tuning works. The single
sharpest test for which side of that split a problem falls on comes from Anyscale's engineering team:
"Fine-tuning typically results in creating a niche model for a niche use-case. The problems that this
approach is effective at are usually those that involve learning the style or form of language rather
than learning new concepts that do not exist in the base knowledge of the foundational model." Their
own experiment made the failure mode concrete: they fine-tuned a
model on a version of Shakespeare with every "Romeo" replaced by "Bob," and the model never learned the
substitution, because "next token prediction loss on a tiny text that replaces Romeo with Bob cannot
change the internals of the neural networks knowledge base." OpenAI's own guidance backs the same
sequencing: "the prompt engineering process may be all you need in order to get great results for your
use case," and a fine-tuning practitioner's guidance from OpenAI puts real numbers on how rarely it's
warranted even among people already building on the API, noting adoption is "less than one percent" and
calling fine-tuning "one of the last tools you should reach for." The concrete data floor is also
smaller than most teams expect: "there should be at least 10 examples," with "between 50 and 100
examples" as a practical recommendation once a task is well-scoped enough that more data helps. This
chapter matters more in 2026 than it would have a year earlier for a second reason: OpenAI is winding
down self-serve fine-tuning, stating plainly that "the platform is no longer accessible to new users,"
which pushes closed-model fine-tuning toward vendor-assisted programs and pushes the accessible,
self-serve path toward the open-weight, parameter-efficient techniques covered starting in Chapter 7.2.

## High-Level Explanation

Before touching any training code, a practicing engineer runs one diagnostic question first: is the
model getting this wrong because it doesn't know something, or because it isn't behaving the way it's
supposed to? A wrong or outdated fact, a citation nobody can verify, an answer that ignores a document
that changed last week, all of those are knowledge problems, and Phase 6 exists because fine-tuning
cannot reliably fix them. A response in the wrong format, a tone that keeps drifting off-brand, an
instruction that needs to be re-explained in every single prompt, those are behavior problems, and only
behavior problems are fine-tuning's actual target. Prompting is tried before either, because it is
the cheapest experiment available and often resolves what looks like a behavior problem without
touching model weights at all. The mistake this chapter is built to prevent is running that diagnostic
backward: reaching for a training run to fix a knowledge gap, discovering months later that the model
still doesn't reliably know the fact, and only then tracing the failure back to a question that should
have been asked on day one.

## Deep-Dive Explanation

**Fine-tuning changes form, not facts, and the mechanism explains why.** Anyscale's engineering team ran
a direct test of what fine-tuning actually teaches a model: they took the works of Shakespeare, replaced
every instance of the name "Romeo" with "Bob," and fine-tuned a model on that modified text expecting it
to learn the substitution as a new fact. It did not. Prompted afterward with "Romeo" in the input, the
model's completion never even produced "Bob" in response, leading the authors to conclude "the model has
not learned about this new concept." Their explanation is a statement about the training objective
itself: "next token prediction loss on a tiny text that replaces Romeo with Bob cannot change the
internals of the neural networks knowledge base." The broader claim generalizes past a name-swap toy
example: "the area where fine tuning doesn't work that well is when it comes to facts and hallucination,"
and their recommended fix for a knowledge problem is exactly the architecture Phase 6 spent nine chapters
building: "have the facts in a separate store, look up those facts and include them in the prompt we send
to the system." Before starting any fine-tuning project, their framing reduces to one question: "does
solving my task require incorporating new concepts into the internal knowledge graph represented inside
the neural network or not." A "yes" points at RAG. A "no," where the task is instead about consistently
reproducing a style, a format, or a narrow behavior, is what fine-tuning is actually built for.

**OpenAI's own sequencing puts prompting first for a concrete, stated reason: it might just be enough.**
Their model-optimization guidance states it directly: "the prompt engineering process may be all you need
in order to get great results for your use case." The benefits fine-tuning offers over prompting alone
are real, but every one of them is a scaling or cost argument rather than a starting-point argument: "you
can use shorter prompts with fewer examples and context data, which saves on token costs at scale and can
be lower latency," "you can provide more example inputs and outputs than could fit within the context
window of a single request," "you can train on proprietary or sensitive data without having to include it
via examples in every request," and "you can train a smaller, cheaper, faster model to excel at a
particular task where a larger model is not cost-effective." Every one of those benefits presumes a
prompt that has already grown long, a cost that is already adding up at volume, or a task that is already
running often enough for a smaller specialized model to be worth building. None of them is a reason to
skip straight to fine-tuning before a prompt has been tried and measured against an eval set at all, the
same golden-dataset discipline Chapter 5.6 already established.

**The practical thresholds are lower, and stricter, than most teams assume.** A fine-tuning practitioner
guide from an OpenAI team member is specific about both ends of this: on the low end, "there should be at
least 10 examples," with "between 50 and 100 examples" as the practical recommendation once a task
justifies the investment; on scope, "ensure that each fine-tuned model is for one task only," rather than
one general-purpose fine-tune expected to improve several unrelated behaviors at once; and on production
fidelity, "keep system and user prompts similar between training and production," since a model
fine-tuned against one prompt template and then served behind a different one in production loses much
of what the fine-tune was supposed to buy. The same source puts a number on how rarely any of this gets
reached for in practice among people already building on a frontier model API: fine-tuning adoption sits
at "less than one percent," described as "one of the last tools you should reach for" only after
prompting and retrieval have been genuinely exhausted, not tried once and abandoned.

**The vendor landscape for fine-tuning itself is shifting in 2026, and that shift changes which path is
actually accessible.** OpenAI's own deprecation notice lays out a phased wind-down of self-serve
fine-tuning: on May 7, 2026, "creating fine-tuning jobs or training is not available to organizations
that have not previously run fine-tuning"; from July 2, 2026, "creating fine-tuning jobs is no longer
available to organizations that have not run inference on a fine-tuned model in the past 60 days"; and
by January 6, 2027, "active existing customers will no longer be able to create new fine-tuning jobs on
this date." Existing fine-tuned models are not being pulled: "inference on fine-tuned models will
continue to be available until the base models are deprecated." The stated reason is a shift toward
higher-touch alternatives: OpenAI describes assisted fine-tuning for teams that need "support setting up
efficient training data pipelines" and a separate custom-trained-models track for organizations with
larger amounts of proprietary data, both replacing what used to be a self-serve API call. For a team
deciding whether fine-tuning is even reachable without a vendor relationship, this matters directly: the
self-serve door on a frontier closed model is narrowing, while the open-weight ecosystem, and the
parameter-efficient techniques Chapter 7.2 covers next, remains the accessible path for a team that wants
to run its own fine-tuning job without waiting on a vendor's assisted program.

Most gaps a team investigates this way never reach the bottom of the tree. The point of running the
diagnostic in order, knowledge-or-behavior first, then prompting, then scope and access, is that each
step is cheaper than the one after it, and skipping straight to the bottom is how teams end up
maintaining a fine-tuned model for a problem a five-minute prompt edit would have solved.
