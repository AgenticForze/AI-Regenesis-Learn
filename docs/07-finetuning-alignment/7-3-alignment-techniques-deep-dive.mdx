## TL;DR

Chapter 7.2 covered how to fine-tune efficiently. This chapter covers a specific kind of fine-tuning:
teaching a model to behave the way people actually want, not just to predict the next token accurately.
OpenAI's InstructGPT paper named the gap directly: "making language models bigger does not inherently
make them better at following a user's intent," and its result is the reason alignment fine-tuning
matters at all: "outputs from the 1.3B parameter InstructGPT model are preferred to outputs from the
175B GPT-3, despite having 100x fewer parameters." That result came from a two-stage recipe, supervised
fine-tuning on human demonstrations followed by reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), where
a reward model trained on human preference rankings guides further training. RLHF works, but it needs a
continuous supply of human preference labels, which is expensive and slow to scale. Anthropic's
Constitutional AI showed a fix: replace the human preference labels with a model's own preference
judgments, calling the result "RL from AI Feedback (RLAIF)." A dedicated comparison study went further
and found this is not a compromise: "RLAIF can achieve performance on-par with using human feedback,
offering a potential solution to the scalability limitations of RLHF," and, in some conditions, it "can
outperform a supervised fine-tuned baseline even when the AI labeler is the same size as the policy."
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) then removed an entire stage of machinery from the pipeline: instead
of training a separate reward model and running reinforcement learning against it, DPO reframes preference
data as "a simple classification loss," reporting that fine-tuning with DPO "exceeds PPO-based RLHF in
ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and
single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train." This chapter covers all
four techniques, in the order they actually build on each other.

## High-Level Explanation

A freshly pretrained model is a fluent but undirected writer: it can continue almost any text convincingly,
but nothing in its training taught it to follow an instruction, refuse a harmful request, or prefer a
helpful answer over a technically-plausible but useless one. Instruction tuning is the first correction: a
short, supervised stage that shows the model many examples of instruction paired with a good response,
teaching it the basic shape of "answer the question you were actually asked." RLHF is the second, deeper
correction, and it works less like a lesson and more like ongoing coaching: instead of just showing more
examples, it has human reviewers compare pairs of the model's own outputs, trains a separate reward model
to predict which one they will prefer, and then uses that reward model to push the underlying model toward
outputs people actually rate higher. RLAIF keeps that same coaching structure but replaces the human
reviewer with a capable AI model itself, trading some of RLHF's grounding in direct human judgment for the
ability to generate preference labels at a scale no human review team could sustain. DPO keeps the
preference data, comparisons of a better response against a worse one, but throws out the coaching
apparatus entirely: no separate reward model, no reinforcement learning loop, just a loss function applied
directly to the pairs.

## Deep-Dive Explanation

**Instruction tuning is the supervised foundation every later stage builds on, and the InstructGPT result
is what proved the whole direction was worth pursuing.** Ouyang et al.'s InstructGPT paper opens with the
core problem: "making language models bigger does not inherently make them better at following a user's
intent." Their fix started with a supervised stage: "we collect a dataset of labeler demonstrations of the
desired model behavior, which we use to fine-tune GPT-3 using supervised learning," before a further
reinforcement-learning stage refined it. The headline result reframed what "better" even means for a
language model: "in human evaluations on our prompt distribution, outputs from the 1.3B parameter
InstructGPT model are preferred to outputs from the 175B GPT-3, despite having 100x fewer parameters."
Raw parameter count was never the bottleneck; alignment to what a user actually wanted was, and a
dramatically smaller, aligned model beat a much larger, unaligned one on exactly that measure. InstructGPT
also reported the fix did not come at a quality tax: "InstructGPT models show improvements in truthfulness
and reductions in toxic output generation while having minimal performance regressions on public NLP
datasets."

**RLHF is the reinforcement-learning stage that took InstructGPT past plain instruction tuning, and it
works by training a reward model to stand in for a human judgment call the underlying model can then be
optimized against.** After the supervised stage, InstructGPT's recipe collects comparison data: "we then
collect a dataset of rankings of model outputs, which we use to further fine-tune this supervised model
using reinforcement learning from human feedback." A reward model learns to predict which of two candidate
responses human labelers would rank higher, and the policy model is then updated with reinforcement
learning, typically PPO, to produce outputs the reward model scores well. This is more expensive and more
operationally complex than instruction tuning alone: it requires an ongoing supply of human comparison
judgments, a separately trained and maintained reward model, and a reinforcement-learning training loop
that is meaningfully harder to stabilize than supervised fine-tuning. The payoff is real, InstructGPT's
100x-fewer-parameters result depended on this stage, not the supervised stage alone, but the human-labeling
requirement is exactly the bottleneck the next two techniques each attack from a different angle.

**RLAIF keeps RLHF's reward-model-plus-reinforcement-learning structure but swaps who supplies the
preference judgments, and the evidence says the swap does not cost as much quality as it might seem to.**
Anthropic's Constitutional AI paper describes a two-stage process where the second stage explicitly
replaces human preference labels: "in the RL phase, we sample from the finetuned model, use a model to
evaluate which of the two samples is better, and then train a preference model from this dataset of AI
preferences. We then train with RL using the preference model as the reward signal, i.e. we use 'RL from
AI Feedback' (RLAIF)." A dedicated later study comparing the two approaches directly found the substitution
holds up: "RLAIF can achieve performance on-par with using human feedback, offering a potential solution to
the scalability limitations of RLHF," with "comparable performance to RLHF" across summarization, helpful
dialogue, and harmless dialogue tasks. One finding is genuinely counterintuitive: "RLAIF can outperform a
supervised fine-tuned baseline even when the AI labeler is the same size as the policy, or even the exact
same checkpoint as the initial policy," meaning a model can meaningfully improve on itself using its own
preference judgments as the training signal, not just borrow judgment from a larger, more capable model.

**DPO does not swap the labeler, it removes the reward model and the reinforcement-learning loop
entirely, collapsing preference optimization into a single supervised loss.** Rafailov et al.'s core
insight is captured in the paper's own title: the language model being trained is "secretly a reward
model" already, so a separate one is not structurally necessary. Their method reframes the preference
optimization problem mathematically, "allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple
classification loss," which directly removes the operational overhead RLHF and RLAIF both still carry:
DPO works "eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or performing significant
hyperparameter tuning." The reported results are not just a simplicity win: "fine-tuning with DPO exceeds
PPO-based RLHF in ability to control sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in
summarization and single-turn dialogue while being substantially simpler to implement and train." DPO
still needs the same kind of preference data RLHF and RLAIF need, pairs of a chosen and a rejected
response, human-labeled or AI-labeled either way; what it removes is everything downstream of that data:
the reward model, the RL loop, and the instability that comes with training one model against another
model's live-updating judgments.

## Hands-On: Compute a DPO Loss by Hand

DPO's core claim is that alignment reduces to "a simple classification loss." The fastest way to see that
is to compute one directly, without any reward model or RL loop in sight.

**Step 1: Start from a preference pair.** A DPO training example is nothing more than a prompt, a chosen
(preferred) response, and a rejected response.

```python
prompt = "Summarize the quarterly report in one sentence."
chosen = "Revenue grew 8% year over year, driven by the enterprise segment."
rejected = "The report has numbers in it and some of them went up this quarter, I think."
```

**Step 2: Get log-probabilities for each response under both the model being trained (the policy) and a
frozen copy of the starting checkpoint (the reference model).** DPO compares how much each model's
preference for the chosen response over the rejected one has shifted, not the raw probabilities alone.

```python
import copy
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

model_name = "distilgpt2"
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(model_name)
policy_model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(model_name)      # this is what training updates
reference_model = copy.deepcopy(policy_model).eval()                 # a frozen snapshot, never updated
for param in reference_model.parameters():
    param.requires_grad_(False)

def sequence_logprob(model, tokenizer, prompt, response, track_grad):
    # Tokenize the prompt and response separately, then concatenate token IDs.
    # Tokenizing "prompt + response" as one string is not safe: byte-level BPE
    # can tokenize differently right at the seam than it would tokenize the
    # prompt alone, which would silently misalign prompt_len against the
    # actual token boundary.
    prompt_ids = tokenizer(prompt, return_tensors="pt").input_ids
    response_ids = tokenizer(response, return_tensors="pt").input_ids
    input_ids = torch.cat([prompt_ids, response_ids], dim=1)
    prompt_len = prompt_ids.shape[1]
    with torch.set_grad_enabled(track_grad):
        logits = model(input_ids).logits
    log_probs = F.log_softmax(logits, dim=-1)
    token_log_probs = log_probs[0, prompt_len - 1:-1].gather(
        1, input_ids[0, prompt_len:].unsqueeze(-1)
    )
    return token_log_probs.sum()

policy_chosen_logp = sequence_logprob(policy_model, tokenizer, prompt, chosen, track_grad=True)
policy_rejected_logp = sequence_logprob(policy_model, tokenizer, prompt, rejected, track_grad=True)
ref_chosen_logp = sequence_logprob(reference_model, tokenizer, prompt, chosen, track_grad=False)
ref_rejected_logp = sequence_logprob(reference_model, tokenizer, prompt, rejected, track_grad=False)
```

**Step 3: Compute the DPO loss.** This is the entire training objective, no reward model, no PPO, no
sampled rollouts.

```python
beta = 0.1  # controls how far the policy is allowed to drift from the reference model

def dpo_loss(policy_chosen_logp, policy_rejected_logp, ref_chosen_logp, ref_rejected_logp, beta):
    policy_logratio = policy_chosen_logp - policy_rejected_logp
    ref_logratio = ref_chosen_logp - ref_rejected_logp
    logits = beta * (policy_logratio - ref_logratio)
    loss = -F.logsigmoid(logits)
    return loss

loss = dpo_loss(policy_chosen_logp, policy_rejected_logp, ref_chosen_logp, ref_rejected_logp, beta)
loss.backward()
# a standard optimizer.step() from here is a complete DPO training step
```

**Step 4: See what the loss is actually rewarding.** The loss shrinks exactly when the policy model
increases its relative preference for `chosen` over `rejected` compared to what the frozen reference model
already had, and grows when the policy drifts the other way. There is no reward model scoring anything,
and no generation happens during this step at all: `sequence_logprob` only ever scores text that was
already written down as part of the preference pair.

```python
print(f"policy prefers chosen by: {(policy_chosen_logp - policy_rejected_logp).item():.3f} log-prob units")
print(f"reference preferred chosen by: {(ref_chosen_logp - ref_rejected_logp).item():.3f} log-prob units")
print(f"DPO loss: {loss.item():.4f}")
```

In a real training run, this loss is computed over a batch of thousands of preference pairs and backpropagated
like any other supervised loss, which is the entire mechanical basis for calling DPO "substantially simpler
to implement and train" than RLHF or RLAIF.

The two paths on the right converge on the same kind of outcome, a model whose outputs people (or an AI
judge) prefer, but they get there through meaningfully different amounts of training infrastructure: RLHF
and RLAIF both carry a reward model and an RL loop; DPO carries neither.
