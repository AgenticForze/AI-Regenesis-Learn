## TL;DR

Chapter 3.4 covered how model families differ architecturally. This chapter covers a separate
question: given a fixed amount of training compute, how should it be spent, and does scaling a model
up ever change its behavior in kind, not just in degree. Kaplan et al. (2020) found that a language
model's test loss falls as a smooth power law in model size, dataset size, and compute, holding
across many orders of magnitude, and their results were initially read as favoring larger models
trained on comparatively less data. Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022) corrected this: compute-optimal
training scales model size and training data roughly together rather than favoring size, and many
contemporary large models, trained under the earlier heuristic, were undertrained relative to their
size. A 70-billion-parameter Chinchilla model trained on proportionally more data outperformed the
280-billion-parameter Gopher model trained under the older heuristic, using the same training compute
budget. Separately, Wei et al. (2022) documented **emergent abilities**: specific capabilities, such
as certain multi-step arithmetic tasks, that appear only above a scale threshold rather than improving
smoothly as scale increases. A widely discussed follow-up (Schaefer et al., 2023) argues that at least
some of this apparent emergence is an artifact of the evaluation metric used rather than a genuine
change in the underlying model, an important and still-debated distinction for anyone deciding how to
evaluate a model before deploying it.

## High-Level Explanation

Think of a fixed training compute budget like a fixed budget for building and stocking a factory: you
can spend it on a bigger factory (more model parameters) or on more raw material run through it (more
training data), and getting the best result for the money means balancing both, not maximizing either
one alone. Scaling laws are the empirical curves that describe how much better a model gets as you add
more of each ingredient, holding roughly steady across a very wide range of scales, which is what makes
them useful for planning a training run before running it. Emergent abilities are a different
phenomenon: rather than a capability gradually improving as scale increases, some specific capabilities
appear to switch on abruptly only once a model crosses a certain scale threshold, similar to water not
gradually becoming "more boiled" as its temperature rises but changing state abruptly at exactly
100 degrees Celsius. Whether that abrupt appearance reflects something real about the underlying model,
or is instead an artifact of how the capability happens to be measured, is a genuinely open, actively
debated question covered in this chapter's Deep-Dive.

## Deep-Dive Explanation

**Kaplan et al.'s scaling laws, and the recommendation they were initially read as supporting.** Kaplan
et al. ([*Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361), 2020) trained
many Transformer language models across a wide range of sizes and measured how test loss depends on
model size (N), dataset size (D), and total training compute (C). They found each relationship follows
a smooth power law, holding consistently across several orders of magnitude, with no evidence of the
returns to scale disappearing at the sizes tested. Their compute-allocation analysis was read at the
time as recommending that, for a fixed compute budget, it is more effective to train a substantially
larger model for comparatively fewer optimization steps than to train a smaller model on proportionally
more data, since the larger model reached a given loss with less total compute. This became a widely
adopted heuristic across the field: scale the model aggressively and treat data quantity as secondary.

**Chinchilla's correction: model size and data size should scale together.** Hoffmann et al.
([*Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556), 2022) retrained
a large family of models systematically varying both model size and training data size, and derived a
corrected compute-optimal frontier: for a fixed compute budget, model size and training data size
should scale roughly in proportion to each other, not favor model size the way the earlier heuristic
suggested. To demonstrate this, they trained **Chinchilla**, a 70-billion-parameter model, on
1.4 trillion tokens, using the same total training compute as **Gopher**, a 280-billion-parameter model
trained on roughly 300 billion tokens under the older heuristic. Chinchilla outperformed Gopher, and
several other contemporary large models trained the same way, across a wide range of downstream
benchmarks, despite having a quarter of Gopher's parameters. This was direct evidence that many
large models trained under the Kaplan-era heuristic were substantially undertrained relative to their
size: additional parameters were not being matched with enough training data to make full use of them.

**Emergent abilities: capabilities that do not improve smoothly with scale.** Wei et al.
([*Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07682), 2022) define an
emergent ability as one that is not present in smaller models, appears in larger models, and cannot be
predicted by extrapolating the performance trend observed at smaller scales. They document concrete
examples across benchmark suites including BIG-Bench, where accuracy on tasks such as certain
multi-step arithmetic problems and specific instruction-following formats stays close to random chance
until model scale crosses a threshold, then rises sharply, a pattern qualitatively different from the
smooth power-law curves in test loss that Kaplan et al. and Chinchilla describe.

**The "mirage" critique: some apparent emergence may be a measurement artifact.** Schaefer, Miranda,
and Koyejo ([*Are Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models a Mirage?*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.15004),
2023) argue that at least some of these observed jumps result from the evaluation metric, not from a
genuine change in the underlying model. Many emergent-ability benchmarks use a discontinuous,
all-or-nothing metric, such as exact-match accuracy on a multi-step problem, where getting any single
token wrong marks the entire answer wrong regardless of how close it was. If the model's underlying
per-token error rate is actually improving smoothly and gradually as scale increases, that smooth
improvement can still produce a sharp, sudden jump in sequence-level exact-match accuracy once the
per-token error rate drops low enough for whole sequences to start succeeding reliably. The paper shows
that switching from a discontinuous metric to a continuous one (such as token-edit distance or
per-token cross-entropy) on the same models and tasks turns several previously "emergent" curves into
smooth, predictable ones. This does not settle the question for every documented case of emergence, and
the debate remains active in the research literature, but it establishes a genuinely important
operational lesson distinct from the scaling-law findings themselves: the choice of evaluation metric
can materially affect whether a capability looks like a sudden phase transition or a gradual
improvement, which matters directly when deciding how to validate a model before deployment.
