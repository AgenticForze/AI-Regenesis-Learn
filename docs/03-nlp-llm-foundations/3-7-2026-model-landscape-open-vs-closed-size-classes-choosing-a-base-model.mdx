## TL;DR

Chapter 3.4 covered how model families differ by architecture and training objective. This chapter
covers a separate, practical question: how a team actually chooses a specific model to deploy, across
three related dimensions. **Open versus closed** describes whether a model's trained weights can be
downloaded and self-hosted (open-weight) or are usable only through a vendor's API (closed). Closed
frontier models, from vendors such as OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google, are accessed entirely through an
API, with the vendor controlling hosting and versioning. Open-weight models, from vendors such as Meta,
DeepSeek, Alibaba's Qwen team, and Mistral, can be downloaded and run on infrastructure the deploying
organization controls, but "open-weight" is not synonymous with "unrestricted": license terms vary
considerably, from genuinely permissive licenses like Apache 2.0 or MIT to conditional community
licenses. Meta's Llama 4 license, for example, requires requesting a separate license from Meta if a
licensee's products or services exceeded 700 million monthly active users as of the Llama 4 release
date. **Size class** is a second,
largely independent dimension: frontier or flagship models offer the highest capability at the highest
cost and latency, mid-size models trade some capability for lower cost and self-hosting feasibility, and
small or edge models trade further capability for the ability to run on limited hardware, including
on-device. Choosing a base model means deciding across openness, size class, and licensing together,
not any one dimension alone.

## High-Level Explanation

Think of choosing a base model like choosing transportation for a specific job. A closed frontier model
is like hiring a chauffeured car: someone else owns, maintains, and upgrades the vehicle, and you pay
per trip, but you can never look under the hood or take it apart to see how it works. An open-weight
model is like buying your own vehicle: you own it outright, can inspect and modify it, and can keep
driving it even if the dealership closes, but the purchase agreement itself may still restrict certain
uses, the way some open-weight licenses restrict deployment past a certain scale. Size class is a
separate choice layered on top, closer to choosing between a delivery truck, a sedan, and a scooter:
each is suited to a different job's constraints of capacity, cost, and where it can actually go, rather
than one simply being a better version of another. The rest of this chapter looks at what "open" and
"restricted" actually mean in a real license, and how the three dimensions combine into a single
deployment decision.

## Deep-Dive Explanation

**"Open-weight" is not the same thing as "open-source," and the difference matters operationally.**
A model is open-weight when its trained parameter values are published for download. That alone says
nothing about whether the training data, training code, or full methodology used to produce those
weights is also published, the standard that a stricter "open-source" definition would require. Most
models marketed as "open" in 2026 release only the weights and inference code, not the training data,
making them open-weight rather than fully open-source in the traditional software sense. This
distinction matters practically: an open-weight model can be inspected, fine-tuned, and self-hosted
regardless of this gap, but a team cannot reproduce or fully audit how it was trained, which is a
different guarantee than what "open source" implies in traditional software licensing.

**Real license terms vary enormously, and the label "open" does not tell you which kind you are
getting.** A genuinely permissive license, such as Apache 2.0 or MIT, imposes no usage restriction:
free commercial use, modification, and redistribution, with no threshold that changes the terms. A
conditional community license imposes usage restrictions above a stated threshold. Meta's Llama 4
license is a concrete, verifiable example: it permits free use, modification, and redistribution of the
weights, but requires the licensee to request a separate license directly from Meta if, as measured on
the Llama 4 version release date, the monthly active users of the licensee's own products or services
was greater than 700 million monthly active users in the calendar month preceding that release date, a
threshold Meta may grant or decline at its sole discretion. The test is anchored to that fixed release
date, not an ongoing condition a licensee must keep monitoring as its own user count changes over time.
This means
"open-weight" describes a spectrum of actual legal freedom, not one fixed category, and the specific
license text has to be checked for the model actually being deployed, not inferred from how the model
is marketed or from another vendor's terms.

**Size class is a distinct axis from openness, and both matter independently to a deployment decision.**
Frontier or flagship models sit at the top of the capability range and typically require either API
access to a vendor's infrastructure or, for open-weight frontier-scale models, a multi-GPU cluster to
self-host. Mid-size models, typically tens of billions of parameters, trade some capability for
meaningfully lower cost, lower latency, and feasible self-hosting on a small number of GPUs. Small or
edge models, sometimes called SLMs, trade further capability for the ability to run on a single
consumer GPU or directly on-device, with no network dependency at all. A smaller model fine-tuned
tightly on one narrow task can sometimes match or exceed a much larger general-purpose frontier model
on that specific task, since the capability a deployment actually needs is task-specific, not simply a
function of parameter count, a point that connects directly to chapter 3.6's discussion of what
additional scale actually buys.

**Choosing a base model means weighing several decision axes together, not optimizing one in
isolation.** In practice a team has to weigh: the task's actual capability requirement, whether data
residency or privacy requirements permit sending data to a third-party API at all, the total cost of
ownership (API per-token pricing versus self-hosting infrastructure and engineering cost), latency
requirements, whether the specific license terms actually permit the intended use at the intended
deployment scale, and the vendor-concentration risk of depending entirely on one API vendor's uptime,
pricing, and deprecation decisions versus owning a downloadable checkpoint that keeps working
regardless of what the original vendor does next. No single axis determines the right answer; a model
that wins on capability can still be the wrong choice on licensing, latency, or vendor risk grounds.
