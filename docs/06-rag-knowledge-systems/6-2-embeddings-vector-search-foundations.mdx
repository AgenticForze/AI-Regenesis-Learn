## TL;DR

Retrieval, the process Chapter 6.1 established RAG depends on, only works if a system can tell which
pieces of a knowledge store are actually relevant to a question, and matching on keywords alone misses
paraphrases, synonyms, and anything phrased differently from the source text. Embeddings solve this by
turning text into a fixed-size vector of numbers positioned so that texts with similar meaning land near
each other in that vector space, an idea that traces back to Mikolov et al.'s word2vec (2013), which
showed it "takes less than a day to learn high quality word vectors from a 1.6 billion words data set"
and reaches "state-of-the-art performance on our test set for measuring syntactic and semantic word
similarities." Reimers and Gurevych's Sentence-BERT (2019) carried the same idea from single words to
full sentences and made it fast enough to use at retrieval scale, cutting the cost of finding the most
similar pair among 10,000 sentences "from 65 hours with BERT / RoBERTa to about 5 seconds," a speedup
of roughly four to five orders of magnitude achieved by fine-tuning a siamese network to produce comparable,
directly-comparable vectors instead of running every pair through the full model. But a vector space
alone does not make retrieval fast: once a knowledge store holds millions or billions of vectors,
finding the nearest ones by brute force runs into the curse of dimensionality, and Indyk and Motwani's
1998 analysis of exactly this problem found that standard indexing structures "provide little
improvement over the brute-force algorithm" as dimensionality grows. Approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) algorithms, HNSW's navigable
graphs, IVF's space-partitioning clusters, and product quantization's compressed codes, exist to trade a
small amount of exactness for search that stays fast and memory-efficient at scale. This chapter covers
both halves of the pipeline 6.1 gestured at: how text becomes a vector, and how the right vector gets
found quickly once there are too many of them to check one by one.

## High-Level Explanation

Picture a library where, instead of shelving books alphabetically, someone has placed every book at a
specific spot in a vast room based purely on what the book is about, so that books on similar topics
end up physically near each other no matter what their titles say. A book on tax law sits close to
other books on tax law even if one is called "Corporate Tax Strategy" and another "A Practitioner's
Guide to Levies," because the placement was never based on the words in the title at all. Embeddings
build exactly this kind of room for text. A piece of text, a word, a sentence, a whole passage, goes
into a trained model and comes out as a list of numbers, a point in a high-dimensional space, and the
model is trained specifically so that texts with similar meaning land near each other in that space
regardless of the specific words each one uses. Finding relevant information then stops being a search
for matching words and becomes a search for nearby points, which is exactly the kind of question a
paraphrase, a synonym, or a differently-worded version of the same idea can still answer correctly.

## Deep-Dive Explanation

**A word's meaning becomes a set of coordinates.** Mikolov et al.'s word2vec (2013) introduced two
neural network architectures for computing continuous vector representations of words directly from
large amounts of raw text, with no manual labeling required. The result was notable for two reasons at
once: it was efficient, "it takes less than a day to learn high quality word vectors from a 1.6 billion
words data set," and it was accurate, reaching "state-of-the-art performance on our test set for
measuring syntactic and semantic word similarities." Words that tend to appear in similar contexts end
up with similar vectors, which is why this class of model can place "corporate" and "levies" near "tax"
even though none of those words look alike as strings of characters. This was the foundational insight
that made everything downstream in this chapter possible: meaning can be represented as coordinates,
and coordinates can be compared mathematically.

**Single words are not enough for retrieval, whole sentences and passages need vectors too.** A
document a retrieval system needs to find is rarely a single word, it is a sentence, a paragraph, or a
whole passage, and averaging word vectors together to represent a sentence loses too much of what the
sentence actually means. Reimers and Gurevych's Sentence-BERT (2019) closed this gap by fine-tuning
BERT with a pooling operation and a siamese or triplet network architecture, so the model learns to
produce a single fixed-size vector per sentence, one where cosine similarity between two such vectors
reflects how similar the two sentences actually are. The payoff was almost entirely about speed at
scale: comparing every pair among a collection of 10,000 sentences with plain BERT "requires about 50
million inference computations (~65 hours)," but with Sentence-BERT the same task takes "about 5
seconds," because the model needs to encode each sentence only once and compare the resulting vectors
directly rather than feeding every pair through the network jointly. Accuracy held up alongside that
speedup: on semantic textual similarity benchmarks, Sentence-BERT scored 11.7 points above InferSent and
5.5 points above the Universal Sentence Encoder, two of the strongest prior sentence-embedding methods.
This is the model family that made embedding an entire knowledge store, then searching it, a practical
idea rather than a theoretical one.

**No embedding model wins everywhere, which is why the field benchmarks them collectively rather than
one task at a time.** Muennighoff et al.'s Massive Text Embedding Benchmark, MTEB (2022), was built
specifically because embeddings were typically evaluated on a narrow slice of tasks, semantic textual
similarity most often, leaving it unclear whether a model that excelled there would do equally well at
clustering, classification, or retrieval. MTEB evaluates across "8 embedding tasks," "58 datasets," and
"112 languages," and its central finding is the reason the benchmark exists at all: "no particular text
embedding method dominates across all tasks." Two of the most widely used commercial models illustrate
the churn this drives. OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large lifted the MIRACL multilingual retrieval
benchmark from 31.4% to 54.9% and MTEB from 61.0% to 64.6% compared to its predecessor, and both new
OpenAI models support Matryoshka Representation Learning, letting a developer shorten an embedding by
passing a `dimensions` parameter without destroying its meaning; OpenAI reports that a shortened
text-embedding-3-large at 256 dimensions still outperforms the older, unshortened ada-002 model at 1,536
dimensions on MTEB. Google's Gemini Embedding model, which "has consistently held a top spot on the
Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB) Multilingual leaderboard," takes a similar approach, defaulting
to 3,072 dimensions with the same Matryoshka technique available to scale down to 1,536 or 768
dimensions, and Google specifically calls out performance across "science, legal, finance, and coding"
domains and over 100 languages. Neither leaderboard position is permanent. Choosing an embedding model
is a benchmarking decision made against the specific mix of tasks a system actually needs, not a
one-time choice of whichever model currently sits at the top of a general-purpose leaderboard.

**Two vectors are compared with a distance or similarity metric, and which one gets used changes what
"closest" means.** Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors and ignores their length
entirely, dot product multiplies the vectors together directly and grows with vector length as well as
angle, and Euclidean distance measures the straight-line distance between the vectors' endpoints. The
choice matters because vector length can itself carry a signal: as Google's own guidance on measuring
embedding similarity puts it, "examples that appear very frequently in the training set (for example,
popular YouTube videos) tend to have embedding vectors with large lengths," so a dot-product search can
end up favoring popular or frequently-referenced content over content that is simply the closest match
in meaning. When every vector in a system is normalized to unit length first, this distinction mostly
disappears: at that point, "all three similarity measures are equivalent because they are proportional
to" the cosine of the angle between them. Most modern text
embedding models are trained and typically used with normalized vectors and cosine similarity for
exactly this reason, though dot product remains a deliberate choice in systems where a document's
inherent "weight" is meant to influence ranking.

**Finding the nearest vector by checking every single one breaks down as a collection grows into the
millions, a problem with a name older than vector search itself.** Richard Bellman coined the phrase
"curse of dimensionality" in 1957, decades before anyone was embedding text, to describe how quickly a
search space grows as dimensions are added to it. Indyk and Motwani's foundational 1998 analysis of
nearest-neighbor search showed exactly how that curse plays out for classic indexing structures, the
kind that work well in two or three dimensions: they stop helping once dimensionality gets large, "for
large d, in theory or in practice, they provide little improvement over the brute-force algorithm" of
comparing a query against every stored vector directly.
Exact nearest-neighbor structures were shown to face a genuine dilemma, forced to choose between "low
preprocessing cost but query time linear in n and d" or a structure with "query time sublinear in n" but
"severely exponential preprocessing" to build. Their proposed way out was to stop insisting on the
exact nearest neighbor and accept an approximate one instead, formally a point whose distance to the
query is within a factor of `(1 + epsilon)` of the true nearest neighbor's distance, on the reasoning
that "determining an epsilon-approximate nearest neighbor for a reasonable value of epsilon...should
suffice for most practical purposes." This is the shift, exact to approximate, that every algorithm
covered in the rest of this chapter depends on.

**Three different families of shortcuts trade a little accuracy for a lot of speed, and each one takes
a different approach to avoiding a full scan.** Graph-based methods, of which Malkov and Yashunin's
Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) is the dominant example, build a multi-layer graph connecting
each vector to a handful of its neighbors, with sparser layers on top for long jumps across the space
and denser layers below for fine-grained refinement; the authors report this construction "allows a
logarithmic complexity scaling" for search, and note the algorithm's structural "similarity...to the
skip list structure allows straightforward balanced distributed implementation." Cluster-based methods,
of which the inverted file index (IVF) is the standard example, partition the vector space into cells
around a set of centroids using a Voronoi-style assignment, so a query only needs to search the cell it
falls into plus a configurable number of neighboring cells, controlled by a parameter typically called
`nprobe`, "the number of cells to search"; raising `nprobe` trades speed for recall, and on a standard
one-million-vector benchmark, IVF was measured delivering roughly 1 to 9 millisecond query times at
0.7 to 0.95 recall against roughly 18 milliseconds for a flat, exhaustive scan of the same data.
Quantization-based methods, of which Jegou, Douze, and Schmid's product quantization (PQ, 2011) is the
foundational technique, compress each vector into a short code by splitting it into `m` subvectors and
quantizing each one independently against a small set of centroids. A companion paper the same year,
Jegou, Tavenard, Douze, and Amsaleg's "Searching in One Billion Vectors," built directly on that
technique and demonstrated it at genuine billion-vector scale: distances get refined "based on short
quantization codes, to avoid reading the full vectors from disk," tested against "a new public dataset
of one billion 128-dimensional vectors," and reported "outperforming FLANN, which was previously shown
to outperform" locality-sensitive hashing, the earlier approximate technique Indyk and Motwani had
introduced. None of these three
families is strictly better than the others: graph-based search tends to win on recall at a given
latency, cluster-based search is simple to reason about and tune, and quantization is what makes
holding billions of vectors in memory affordable in the first place.

**In production these techniques are usually combined, not chosen in isolation, and the choice is
tracked empirically rather than assumed.** A system might partition vectors with IVF and then compress
each cell's contents with product quantization to save memory, or build an HNSW graph over quantized
codes to get graph-based recall at a fraction of the memory cost of storing full vectors. Benchmarking
projects such as ANN-Benchmarks exist precisely because the right combination depends on the specific
recall target, latency budget, and memory ceiling of a given system, not on which algorithm sounds most
sophisticated on paper. That combination, along with the question of which underlying database or
library actually implements it, is exactly what Chapter 6.3 picks up next.
