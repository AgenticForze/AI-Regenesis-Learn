## TL;DR

A pretrained language model genuinely does store knowledge in its weights, and it stores more of it
than most people expect: Petroni et al. (2019) documented "the surprisingly strong ability of these
models to recall factual knowledge without any fine-tuning," and Roberts et al. (2020) showed that
this ability "scales with model size" enough to perform "competitively with open-domain systems that
explicitly retrieve answers from an external knowledge source." That parametric knowledge is real, but
it is also bounded in four specific ways this chapter names directly: it is frozen at whatever moment
training stopped, it carries no source a reader can check, it is learned unevenly (Petroni again:
"certain types of factual knowledge are learned much more readily than others"), and updating it is
neither cheap nor safe, since naive retraining risks catastrophic forgetting and even surgical editing
techniques operate on knowledge that Meng et al. (2022) found localized in specific "middle-layer
feed-forward modules," not stored as a simple lookup table. Retrieval-augmented generation exists
because of exactly this gap. Lewis et al.'s foundational 2020 paper is blunt about it: a pretrained
model's "ability to access and precisely manipulate knowledge is still limited," and "providing
provenance for their decisions and updating their world knowledge remain open research problems." RAG
answers all four limits at once by pairing the model's parametric memory with a non-parametric,
swappable, citable knowledge store queried fresh at every request. This chapter establishes why that
pairing matters before Phase 6's later chapters build the retrieval pipeline itself.

## High-Level Explanation

Picture a person who read an enormous library once, years ago, and then walked away from it for good.
Ask them almost anything and they will answer fluently, often correctly, because they really did read
that much. But they cannot walk back into the library to check a fact, they have no idea which shelf
a particular claim came from, they read some sections of that library far more carefully than others,
and if you want them to learn something new, the only option is another multi-year reading project
that risks scrambling what they already knew. A pretrained language model's parametric knowledge works
the same way: real, broad, genuinely useful, and shaped by exactly those same four limits. Retrieval-
augmented generation does not try to fix the person's memory. It hands them a live phone line to the
library itself, so they can look something up, read the actual page, and tell you exactly where they
found it, every time you ask.

## Deep-Dive Explanation

**Parametric knowledge is real, and it is not evenly distributed.** Petroni et al.'s "Language Models
as Knowledge Bases?" (2019) tested this directly by querying BERT with fill-in-the-blank factual
statements instead of a structured knowledge base, and found that "BERT contains relational knowledge
competitive with traditional NLP methods that have some access to oracle knowledge," with the added
advantage that language models "require no schema engineering, allow practitioners to query about an
open class of relations, are easy to extend to more data, and require no human supervision to train."
Roberts et al. (2020) pushed the same question further and found the effect scales: a large enough
pretrained model, fine-tuned only to answer questions from what it already knows, "performs
competitively with open-domain systems that explicitly retrieve answers from an external knowledge
source." Neither paper found this coverage to be uniform, though. Petroni's own results showed that
"certain types of factual knowledge are learned much more readily than others by standard language
model pretraining approaches," a direct consequence of how pretraining data is distributed: a fact
repeated across thousands of web pages gets learned solidly, and a fact that appeared once, in one
obscure document, gets learned poorly or not at all.

**That knowledge is frozen the moment training data collection stops.** Every model ships with a
training cutoff, the last date its pretraining corpus reflects, and nothing about the model's
architecture lets it look past that date on its own. A model asked about an event, a price, a
regulation, or a policy that changed after its cutoff will answer anyway, drawing on whatever it
learned about the state of the world before that point, with no internal signal that the world has
since moved on. This is the most visible of the four limits, and it is also the one most directly
responsible for confident, plausible-sounding wrong answers.

</figure>

**A fact stored in weights carries no pointer back to where it came from.** This is the limit Lewis et
al.'s "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks" (2020) names most precisely,
and it is the paper that gave RAG its name. Its motivating claim: large pretrained models' "ability to
access and precisely manipulate knowledge is still limited, and hence on knowledge-intensive tasks,
their performance lags behind task-specific architectures," and more fundamentally, "providing
provenance for their decisions and updating their world knowledge remain open research problems." Ask
a fine-tuned model why it believes a specific figure or claim, and there is no document it can point
to, because none was ever attached to that fact in the first place; the number and the sentence it
appeared in were both compressed into the same undifferentiated set of weights. Lewis et al.'s proposed
fix combines "a pre-trained seq2seq model" as parametric memory with "a dense vector index of
Wikipedia, accessed with a pre-trained neural retriever" as non-parametric memory, so that every
generated answer can be traced back to the specific passage that informed it. Google Cloud's framing
of the same idea is more direct still: RAG produces "grounded generation," and doing so is "more
accurate, up-to-date, and relevant" precisely because "LLMs are limited to their pre-trained data,"
which "leads to outdated and potentially inaccurate responses."

**Updating what a model knows is neither cheap nor safe.** The obvious fix for stale or missing
knowledge, retrain the model on new data, runs into a problem with a specific name: catastrophic
forgetting. Kirkpatrick et al. (2017) describe the phenomenon plainly, noting that "it has been widely
thought that catastrophic forgetting is an inevitable feature of connectionist models" learning new
information in sequence, meaning a network can lose what it already knew while being trained on
something new, unless the training procedure is specifically designed to protect the old knowledge. A
more surgical alternative, editing a specific fact directly in the model's weights, sounds simpler but
is not: Meng et al.'s "Locating and Editing Factual Associations in GPT" (2022) found that factual
associations "correspond to localized, directly-editable computations" concentrated in "a distinct set
of steps in middle-layer feed-forward modules," meaning a single fact lives in a specific, identifiable
place rather than a table you can look up and overwrite. Editing it well enough that the new fact
sticks, without corrupting nearby facts or breaking the model's general behavior, is itself an active
research problem; Meng et al.'s own method (ROME) is notable specifically because it "simultaneously
maintains both specificity and generalization, whereas other methods sacrifice one or another," which
would not be a notable result if the underlying problem were easy.

**RAG addresses all four limits by adding a second, non-parametric memory.** Anthropic's own glossary
definition captures the mechanism concisely: RAG "combines information retrieval with language model
generation to improve the accuracy and relevance of the generated text, and to better ground the
model's response in evidence," where "the data is retrieved at runtime when a query is sent to the
model" and "relevant information first must be retrieved from the knowledge base based on the input
prompt, and then passed to the model along with the original query." Because that knowledge base lives
outside the model's weights, it can be updated the moment a source document changes, answering the
staleness problem; because retrieval returns actual passages, an answer can cite exactly which passage
it drew on, answering the provenance problem; because the knowledge base can be built and curated
directly, coverage no longer depends on how often a fact happened to appear on the open web, answering
the uneven-coverage problem; and because updating the knowledge base means adding or replacing a
document rather than retraining or surgically editing weights, updates carry none of fine-tuning's
forgetting risk. AWS's framing puts the payoff simply: RAG "provides the model with the context it
needs to produce accurate and useful output for your specific use case," which AWS calls "a pragmatic
and effective approach to using LLMs in an enterprise." None of this makes RAG free. It introduces a
retrieval pipeline, an index that must be built and maintained, and new failure modes of its own,
covered in Phase 6's later chapters; this chapter's claim is narrower and more specific, that the four
limits above are real, well-documented, and worth solving.

## Reference Architecture
